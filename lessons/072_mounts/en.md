# The Mounts and Steeds of the People of Jannah

## Lesson Objectives and Outcomes

After this lesson, the learner will be able to:

- Recite the words of Allah, `In it you will have whatever your souls desire, and in it you will have whatever you ask for` (Fussilat 41:31-32), present how Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di explain them, and show that Abu Nu'aym made this ayah the heading of his chapter on the riding animals and mounts of the people of Jannah.
- Narrate the hadith of Abu Mas'ud al-Ansari in Sahih Muslim (1892): the man who brought a haltered she-camel in the way of Allah and was promised seven hundred she-camels, every one of them haltered; together with an-Nawawi's preferred reading that these are camels in Jannah that he will ride wherever he wishes, and the authentic narration of Ibn Mas'ud: "in Jannah."
- Explain that animals for riding are a blessing in this world, given for riding and for beauty, as in the ayah `And horses, mules and donkeys, for you to ride and as adornment` (an-Nahl 16:8) and Ibn Kathir's explanation of it.
- Distinguish what is established about the mounts of Jannah from popular reports that are not established, such as the ruby horse that flies with its rider, and set those reports aside rather than rely on them.
- Carry out the "My Ride Ticket in the Way of Allah" activity: lending something that carries the learner, or carries a load for them, to meet someone else's real need, then writing a dated ride ticket and a reflection.
- Close the day with a lesson-authored du'a asking Allah to accept our giving, multiply it, and store it up for us as treasure in Jannah, and to give us there whatever we ask of its delights.

## Academic Section for Adults

<!-- reader:start audience="adults" estimated_minutes="6.5" -->

<!-- unit:start id="adults.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

People grow attached to whatever carries them from place to place: a horse or a camel in times gone by, a bicycle or a car today. Allah reminds His servants of His favor in giving them animals to ride and to take pride in. Are there mounts in Jannah, then? And what can we rightly say about them?

This topic is a clear case of why we must separate what is established from what is merely well known. Abu Nu'aym, in *Sifat al-Jannah*, gave a chapter the heading "The riding animals, mounts and horses of its people," and took as its proof the words of Allah, `and in it you will have whatever you ask for`;[^4] and Ibn al-Qayyim devoted a chapter of *Hadi al-Arwah* to the riding animals, horses and mounts of the people of Jannah. Yet most of the detailed hadiths on this subject, such as the horse of red ruby that flies its rider wherever he wishes, have been judged weak by the hadith scholars, or shown to have confused chains, so this lesson does not rely on them.[^11]

Three truths emerge from the established texts:

1. **The general principle:** the people of Jannah will have in it everything their souls desire and everything they ask for, and that includes the riding they love, as Abu Nu'aym understood when he placed the ayah at the head of his chapter. We do this without asserting any description for which no established text has come.
2. **An authentic text about camels:** the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, promised a man who brought a haltered she-camel in the way of Allah seven hundred she-camels, every one of them haltered. An-Nawawi preferred to take this at face value, and the authentic narration of Ibn Mas'ud says: "in Jannah."
3. **A mount in this world is a blessing and a trust:** Allah created horses, mules and donkeys for riding and for beauty, and whoever gives up his mount in the way of Allah has his gift multiplied.

## Central Evidence

<!-- evidence:start id="quran-41-31-32" kind="quran" mode="canonical" -->

### "And in It You Will Have Whatever You Ask For"

> **{نَحْنُ أَوْلِيَاؤُكُمْ فِي الْحَيَاةِ الدُّنْيَا وَفِي الْآخِرَةِ ۖ وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا مَا تَشْتَهِي أَنْفُسُكُمْ وَلَكُمْ فِيهَا مَا تَدَّعُونَ ۝ نُزُلًا مِنْ غَفُورٍ رَحِيمٍ}** [فصلت: ٣١-٣٢][^1]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> **"We are your protecting friends in the life of this world and in the Hereafter. In it you will have whatever your souls desire, and in it you will have whatever you ask for: a hospitable welcome from One who is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."** (Fussilat 41:31-32)[^1]

#### Scholarly Explanation

These are the words of the angels to those who said "Our Lord is Allah" and then stood firm, as the preceding ayah describes. On `and in it you will have whatever you ask for`, Ibn Kathir said: "That is, whatever you ask for, you will find; it will be brought before you, [that is,] just as you chose."[^2] As-Sa'di said: "That is, you will ask for everything your will reaches toward, and you will seek every kind of pleasure and delight, of what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and has never occurred to any human heart."[^3] Abu Nu'aym made this ayah the heading of his chapter "On the riding animals, mounts and horses of its people."[^4]

#### Lesson Explanation

The ayah is general: it names neither horse nor camel, and that generality is the safe ground to stand on. Whoever loves to ride will find in Jannah whatever he desires, in a form that Allah knows.

<!-- evidence:end -->

<!-- evidence:start id="muslim-1892" kind="hadith" mode="canonical" -->

### "For It You Will Have, on the Day of Resurrection, Seven Hundred She-Camels"

> عَنْ أَبِي مَسْعُودٍ الْأَنْصَارِيِّ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: **جَاءَ رَجُلٌ بِنَاقَةٍ مَخْطُومَةٍ فَقَالَ: هَذِهِ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ. فَقَالَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ صلى الله عليه وسلم: «لَكَ بِهَا يَوْمَ الْقِيَامَةِ سَبْعُمِائَةِ نَاقَةٍ كُلُّهَا مَخْطُومَةٌ»**.[^7]
>
> وفي رواية عبد الله بن مسعود رضي الله عنه: **جَاءَ رَجُلٌ بِنَاقَةٍ مَخْطُومَةٍ فَقَالَ: يَا رَسُولَ اللَّهِ، هَذِهِ النَّاقَةُ فِي سَبِيلِ اللَّهِ. فَقَالَ: «لَكَ بِهَا سَبْعُمِائَةِ نَاقَةٍ مَخْطُومَةٍ فِي الْجَنَّةِ»**.[^10]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> On the authority of Abu Mas'ud al-Ansari, may Allah be pleased with him, who said: **A man came with a she-camel fitted with a halter and said, "This is in the way of Allah." The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "For it you will have, on the Day of Resurrection, seven hundred she-camels, every one of them haltered."**[^7]
>
> In the narration of 'Abdullah ibn Mas'ud, may Allah be pleased with him: **A man came with a haltered she-camel and said, "O Messenger of Allah, this she-camel is in the way of Allah." He said: "For it you will have seven hundred haltered she-camels in Jannah."**[^10]

#### Scholarly Explanation

An-Nawawi said: "*Makhtumah* means that it has a *khitam*, which is close in meaning to a *zimam*," that is, a halter or lead rope. Then he said: "It has been said that it may mean he will have the reward of seven hundred she-camels; or it may be taken at face value, so that he will have seven hundred in Jannah in return for it, each one of them haltered, which he will ride wherever he wishes for his enjoyment, as has come concerning the horses of Jannah and its fine camels. This second possibility is the more evident one. And Allah knows best."[^8] Abu al-'Abbas al-Qurtubi said: "This good deed is among those multiplied up to seven hundredfold, which is the highest of the specified numbers to which good deeds are multiplied," and he cited the ayah of al-Baqarah about the grain that sprouted seven ears.[^9]

#### Lesson Explanation

The man gave a single camel, ready to be led by its halter, and the reward came in kind: camels "every one of them haltered," multiplied seven hundred times over. Whether the commentators read this as reward or as real camels is a respected difference of opinion, and Ibn Mas'ud's narration strengthens the reading an-Nawawi preferred.

<!-- evidence:end -->

<!-- evidence:start id="quran-16-8" kind="quran" mode="canonical" -->

### "For You to Ride and as Adornment"

> **{وَالْخَيْلَ وَالْبِغَالَ وَالْحَمِيرَ لِتَرْكَبُوهَا وَزِينَةً ۚ وَيَخْلُقُ مَا لَا تَعْلَمُونَ}** [النحل: ٨][^5]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> **"And horses, mules and donkeys, for you to ride and as adornment. And He creates what you do not know."** (an-Nahl 16:8)[^5]

#### Scholarly Explanation

Ibn Kathir said: "This is another kind of what He, Blessed and Exalted is He, has created for His servants, a favor He reminds them of: horses, mules and donkeys, which He made for riding and for beauty, and those are the greatest purposes they serve."[^6]

#### Lesson Explanation

The mounts of this world are a gift from Allah that call for gratitude, and part of that gratitude is to put them to use in doing good. This lesson does not read the words `And He creates what you do not know` as a reference to the mounts of Jannah, since the commentators quoted here do not mention that.

<!-- evidence:end -->

### What, Then, Do We Say About the Mounts of Jannah?

We say with confidence: the people of Jannah will have whatever their souls desire and whatever they ask for, and an authentic promise of haltered she-camels was made to a man who gave a she-camel in charity. We do not say, as a certainty, that there is a winged horse made of ruby, and we do not attribute that to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, as an established hadith: at-Tirmidhi himself judged that its best-known chain is *mursal*, and he declared another chain weak because of Abu Sawrah.[^11] The established general promise is more than enough, with no need for weak details.

## Questions for Understanding and Reflection

1. How did Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di explain `and in it you will have whatever you ask for`? And why does this general statement serve as evidence for the mounts of the people of Jannah?
2. What two possibilities did an-Nawawi mention for the seven hundred she-camels? Which did he prefer? And how does Ibn Mas'ud's narration strengthen his preference?
3. How does the man's gift correspond to his reward in the words "every one of them haltered"?
4. Why does the lesson not rely on the hadith of the ruby horse that flies with its rider? And what remains established once it is set aside?
5. What is your "mount" today? And how can giving it in the way of Allah be a way of thanking Him for the blessing in `for you to ride and as adornment`?

### Activity: My Ride Ticket in the Way of Allah

<!-- activity:start audience="adults" concept_id="lesson.072.activity.lend-my-mount-ride-ticket" -->

1. Identify your "mount": whatever carries you, or carries a load for you, in your day, such as a car, a bicycle, a transit pass, a seat, or your own strength to carry things.
2. Within seven days, lend your mount at least once to meet someone else's real need, seeking the pleasure of Allah: drive an elderly neighbor to an appointment, carry someone's groceries, lend your bicycle, or give up your seat. Before you do it, say quietly to yourself: "This is in the way of Allah."
3. Afterward, write a dated "ride ticket": who or what was carried, from where to where, your intention, and what it was like to wait or walk without your mount. Then add two lines: what is established about the mounts of Jannah, and what you have learned to set aside because it is not established.

### A True Report: The Man and His Camel

<!-- story:start audience="4-7" role="authenticated" type="hadith" source_id="muslim-1892" authenticated="true" -->

<!-- retelling:start source_id="muslim-1892" audience="4-7" -->

**This is a true report narrated by Imam Muslim. It is not a made-up story.**

In the time of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, people traveled on camels.

A man came leading his she-camel. There was a rope on its nose to lead it with, called a *khitam*. His camel was all ready to ride.

The man said, "This is in the way of Allah," which means: I am giving it for Allah.[^7]

The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "For it you will have, on the Day of Resurrection, seven hundred she-camels, every one of them haltered." That means: in its place you will have seven hundred camels, every one of them ready with its rope.[^7]

Some scholars thought it most likely that these are camels in Jannah, and that he will ride them wherever he wishes.[^8]

Allah says: `And horses, mules and donkeys, for you to ride and as adornment`.[^5] The things we ride are a blessing from Allah, and we thank Him for them. And in Jannah? ### An Imagined Teaching Story: Sami and the New Bike

<!-- story:start audience="8-12" role="primary" type="creative" source_id="lesson-authored:lesson.072.primary" authenticated="false" -->

**This is an imagined teaching story. It is not a hadith and not a historical report.**

Sami, who was ten, had saved his allowance for months to buy a brand-new blue bike. He couldn't wait for Saturday's ride around the big park with his friends.

On Thursday he saw his friend Badr walking slowly, weighed down by two heavy bags. "My bike's broken," Badr said, "and my grandma's sick. I go to the market every day for her things. It's a long way."

Sami said nothing. He knew Badr needed a bike for a whole week, until his was fixed. But Sami's bike was brand new, Saturday was coming, and it might get scratched or dirty.

That evening Sami and his mom read the hadith of the man who brought his she-camel, saying, "This is in the way of Allah," and the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, promised him seven hundred she-camels. His mom said, "He gave the most precious thing he had to ride, and Allah gave him back many times over."

Sami fell asleep thinking. In the morning, he wheeled his bike over to Badr's house. "Take it this week for your grandma's errands. I'll walk to school."

"What about the ride on Saturday?" Badr asked.

Sami grinned. "This time I'll do the ride on foot."

On Saturday his friends rode, and Sami jogged behind them, out of breath and laughing. At the end of the week Badr brought the bike back, with a small scratch on the fender. Sami felt a little sting. Then he remembered his intention and said no word of blame to his friend.

Two days later, Badr's grandmother came herself, leaning on her cane. "May Allah reward you with good, my son," she said. "My things reached me every single day." And she made a long du'a for him.

Sami looked at the scratch and thought, "That's not a scratch. That's the mark of a ride that was for Allah."

### A True Report: One Camel and Seven Hundred Camels

<!-- story:start audience="8-12" role="authenticated" type="hadith" source_id="muslim-1892" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true report narrated by Muslim from Abu Mas'ud al-Ansari, may Allah be pleased with him. It is not a made-up story.**

In the time of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, a she-camel was the most valuable thing a traveler owned: he rode it, and it carried his things.

A man came with a haltered she-camel: its *khitam*, the rope it is led by, was on its nose, ready for the road. He said: "This is in the way of Allah."[^7]

<!-- terminology:start source_id="muslim-1892" -->

- **`A haltered she-camel (naqah makhtumah)`** — a she-camel with a *khitam* on its nose, a rope like a lead rope for leading it.[^8]
- **`In the way of Allah (fi sabil Allah)`** — any sincere deed done to draw closer to Allah; the phrase is most often used for jihad.[^7]
- **`Multiplying (al-mudha'afah)`** — Allah rewarding a good deed many times over; in this hadith it reached seven hundred.[^9]
- **`Whatever you ask for (ma tadda'un)`** — what you ask for in Jannah, found ready before you, just as you chose.[^2]

## For Teens, Ages 13+

<!-- reader:start audience="13+" estimated_minutes="6.5" -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

About the mounts of Jannah there is plenty of talk and little that is established. Two things are established. The first is the general promise in the words of Allah, `In it you will have whatever your souls desire, and in it you will have whatever you ask for`,[^1] which as-Sa'di explained as "everything your will reaches toward."[^3] The second is an authentic hadith about a man who gave a haltered she-camel in charity and was promised seven hundred she-camels, which an-Nawawi thought most likely to be in Jannah, for him to ride wherever he wishes.[^8] As for the horse of red ruby that flies with its rider: famous as it is, the hadith scholars have judged its chains weak, and at-Tirmidhi judged that the best known of them is *mursal*, so we do not rely on it.[^11] The mounts of this world are a gift, `for you to ride and as adornment`,[^5] and we thank Allah for them by using them for good.

<!-- unit:end -->

### An Imagined Teaching Story: Nour and the Viral Clip

<!-- story:start audience="13+" role="primary" type="creative" source_id="lesson-authored:lesson.072.primary" authenticated="false" -->

**This is an imagined teaching story. It is not a hadith and not a historical report.**

Nour is fifteen, and every Saturday she takes riding lessons at a stable near her home. Her friend Jana loves horses even more than she does, but her family can't afford the stable's fees, so Jana makes do with horse pictures on her phone.

In the class group chat, a popular classmate posted an AI-generated clip: a gleaming red horse with wings, flying between glowing trees. Under it, the caption read: "Authentic hadith: in Jannah there's a ruby horse that flies you anywhere you want! Share with ten people or you're stingy with good."

Nour liked the clip, and she thought about sending it to Jana to make her happy. But that "share or else" line bothered her.

She checked a reliable hadith encyclopedia website: at-Tirmidhi had narrated this hadith, the hadith scholars had judged it weak, and at-Tirmidhi himself had judged it *mursal*.[^11] She felt a small pang of disappointment; she had wanted it to be true.

She hesitated. If she corrected the classmate publicly, she might look like a know-it-all. If she stayed quiet, the clip would keep spreading in the name of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him.

She wrote in the group, calmly: "The clip is beautiful, but I looked it up: the scholars judged this hadith weak, so we shouldn't attribute it to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him. What's authentic is even more beautiful: `In it you will have whatever your souls desire`, and the hadith in Muslim about the man who gave a she-camel in charity and was promised seven hundred."

A few replies trickled in. Then the classmate wrote, "I didn't know that, thanks," and deleted the "share or else" line.

But a question stayed in Nour's heart. The owner of the she-camel had given the very thing he rode. So what would she give?

She called her riding instructor and asked to give her Saturday lesson to her friend. That Saturday, Nour stood at the fence and watched Jana stroke the horse's neck with a trembling hand, then laugh a laugh Nour had never heard from her before.

That night Nour wrote: "I didn't ride today, but I didn't lose a thing. And for what I hope for in Jannah, what's authentic is enough for me."

### A True Report: "This Is in the Way of Allah"

<!-- story:start audience="13+" role="authenticated" type="hadith" source_id="muslim-1892" authenticated="true" -->

<!-- retelling:start source_id="muslim-1892" audience="13+" -->

**This is a true report in Sahih Muslim from Abu Mas'ud al-Ansari, may Allah be pleased with him. It is not an imagined scene.**

The man did not come with money to be counted out. He came with a "haltered" she-camel: its *khitam* on its nose, ready for whoever would lead it. And he said just a few words: "This is in the way of Allah."[^7]

The answer came: "For it you will have, on the Day of Resurrection, seven hundred she-camels, every one of them haltered."[^7] And in the authentic narration of Ibn Mas'ud: "in Jannah."[^10]

The commentators paused over the words. An-Nawawi said the meaning could be the reward of seven hundred she-camels, or camels in Jannah "which he will ride wherever he wishes for his enjoyment," and he preferred the second.[^8] Al-Qurtubi said that this good deed "is among those multiplied up to seven hundredfold."[^9]

There are two lessons in this scene. Real giving comes from what you need and love, not from whatever you have left over. And the reward comes in kind: one camel ready to go is answered by seven hundred, all ready to go.

<!-- retelling:end -->

<!-- story:end -->

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.terms" kind="terms" -->

<!-- terminology:start source_id="muslim-1892" -->

- **`Haltered (makhtumah)`** — wearing its *khitam*, something like a lead rope; that is, ready to be led and ridden.[^8]
- **`Whatever you ask for (ma tadda'un)`** — everything you ask for in Jannah, found ready before you, just as you chose.[^2]
- **`Weak hadith (hadith da'if)`** — a hadith whose chain does not meet the conditions for acceptance, so it is not attributed to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, as a certainty.[^11]
- **`Mursal`** — a hadith that a Successor (*tabi'i*) attributes directly to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, without naming the Companion in between; most hadith scholars count it as a kind of weak hadith.[^11]

<!-- terminology:end -->

### Discussion Questions

1. Why did the line "Share or you're stingy" bother Nour? And what is the danger in spreading a hadith in the name of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, without checking it?
2. How did Nour correct the mistake without embarrassing her classmate? And what would you have written?
3. What are the two possible meanings of "seven hundred she-camels"? And why did an-Nawawi prefer one of them?
4. What connects the gift of the she-camel's owner with Nour giving up her lesson?
5. Does setting aside weak reports lessen your longing for Jannah? Discuss in light of `and in it you will have whatever you ask for`.

### Activity: My Ride Ticket in the Way of Allah

<!-- activity:start audience="13+" concept_id="lesson.072.activity.lend-my-mount-ride-ticket" -->

1. Identify your real mount: your bike, your transit pass, your seat, your spot in an activity you love, or your time and strength for carrying other people and their things.
2. Within seven days, lend it once to meet someone else's real need, with a sincere intention, and without filming or posting it.
3. In your private notebook, write a "ride ticket": the date, who or what was carried, from where to where, and what it cost you. Then add two lines: what is established about the mounts of Jannah, and what you have learned to check before you share it.

### Bedtime Du'a

<!-- bedtime-dua:start audience="13+" id="lesson.072.dua.multiply-our-giving-treasure-in-jannah" provenance="lesson_authored" source_id="" attribution="not_prophetic" -->

**Lesson-authored thematic du'a; not attributed to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him.**

> اللَّهُمَّ تَقَبَّلْ مِنَّا مَا بَذَلْنَاهُ لَكَ، وَضَاعِفْهُ لَنَا أَضْعَافًا كَثِيرَةً، وَاجْعَلْهُ ذُخْرًا لَنَا فِي الْجَنَّةِ، وَأَعْطِنَا فِيهَا مِنْ نَعِيمِهَا مَا نَطْلُبُ.

This du'a joins today's deeds to tomorrow's hope: that Allah accept what we have given and multiply it, as He promised the owner of the she-camel; that He store it up for us as treasure in Jannah; and that He give us there whatever we ask for, as He promised: `and in it you will have whatever you ask for`.[^1]

## Detailed Teaching Plans

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="adults" minutes="60" -->

### Adults — 60 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The learner recites the two ayat of Fussilat (41:31-32) and presents the explanations of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di; narrates the hadith of Abu Mas'ud (Muslim 1892) and the narration of Ibn Mas'ud; explains an-Nawawi's two possibilities and his preference, and al-Qurtubi's words on multiplying; shows the blessing of riding animals in the ayah of an-Nahl (16:8); distinguishes what is established from what is not about the mounts of Jannah; and begins the "My Ride Ticket in the Way of Allah" activity.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** A copy of the lesson; a mushaf; a board with two columns, "Established: we say it with confidence" and "Popular: we do not rely on it"; a printed "ride ticket" template (date, who or what was carried, from where to where, intention, what it was like to be without the mount); a short rope to show what a *khitam* is.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher reviews the tafsir of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di on Fussilat 41:31, Ibn Kathir's tafsir on an-Nahl 16:8, the commentaries of an-Nawawi and al-Qurtubi on Muslim 1892, and chapter fifty-eight of *Hadi al-Arwah* with the editor's notes, along with the hadith scholars' rulings on at-Tirmidhi 2543 and 2544.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** The teacher asks: "What is the first picture that comes to mind when you think of the mounts of Jannah? And where did it come from?" The answers are written on the board without any judgment on them.

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 20 minutes:** Read the two ayat of Fussilat with both tafsirs, and point out that Abu Nu'aym made the ayah the heading of his chapter on riding animals. Then read the hadith of Abu Mas'ud and the narration of Ibn Mas'ud, explain the *khitam* using the rope, and present an-Nawawi's two possibilities, his preference, and al-Qurtubi's words. Then read the ayah of an-Nahl with Ibn Kathir's tafsir.

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 10 minutes:** The teacher returns to the answers from the opening, and the learners sort them into the two columns. Explain that at-Tirmidhi judged the hadith of Buraydah to be *mursal* and declared the hadith of Abu Ayyub weak because of Abu Sawrah, and that Ibn al-Qayyim pointed out 'Alqamah ibn Marthad's inconsistency in narrating it, without reading out the texts of the weak reports as though they were proof. Discuss: What do we lose when we set aside the weak? And what remains?

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 15 minutes:** Each learner identifies their "mount" and writes a plan for lending it within seven days: to whom, when, and to meet what need, as in the activity unit. They practice filling in the ticket template with an example from their own past.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 10 minutes:** Each learner writes down: Ibn Kathir's meaning of `whatever you ask for`, the text of Muslim 1892, an-Nawawi's two possibilities, and one sentence they would use to correct someone who attributed the ruby-horse hadith to the Prophet. The group agrees that anyone who wishes can share their ticket after a week.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For beginners, the ayah of Fussilat and the hadith in Muslim are enough. Advanced learners are asked to read chapter fifty-eight of *Hadi al-Arwah* with its notes and to summarize the defects of the chains in a table: the chain, the defect, and who identified it.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** None of the unestablished descriptions of the horses of Jannah, the ruby, the wings and the flying, is attributed to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, as a certainty.[^11] The difference of opinion over the meaning of the seven hundred is presented faithfully, since it is a respected difference among the commentators. The words `And He creates what you do not know` are not applied to the mounts of Jannah without a scholarly source. When lending, safety, permission and the law are respected, so no vehicle is lent to someone who cannot handle it properly. No one is called stingy for not lending.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="4-7" minutes="25" -->

### Children Ages 4-7 — 25 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The child says that Allah made horses for us to ride, and that the people of Jannah will have everything they love there; retells the report of the man who gave his she-camel and was promised seven hundred; gives up a turn on a riding toy or carries something for someone else; and repeats the lesson's du'a.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** A small bike or a toy car; a short rope and a toy camel or a picture of a she-camel; card stock for a small ticket; coloring pencils; the du'a card.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The educator practices telling Reem's story and retelling the true report in simple words, and prepares a blank ticket template with two boxes drawn on it: "Who rode?" and "What did I carry?"

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 3 minutes:** Ask the child: "What do you love to ride? And if your friend came and wanted it, what would you do?"

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Reading the Evidence — 7 minutes:** Tell Reem's story, then say, "And this is a true report," and retell the report of the man and his she-camel, tying the rope to the toy camel to show what a *khitam* is. The child counts on their fingers: "One camel... and seven hundred!"

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 4 minutes:** The children act out the scene: one child has the toy, and another is waiting. The first says, "You go first, this is for Allah," and walks beside the other. Then they swap roles.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 8 minutes:** Each child draws their ticket as in the activity unit, and the educator explains to the parents how to complete it at home and write in the date.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 3 minutes:** Ask the child: "What did the man give for Allah?" "How many camels was he promised?" and "What does Allah give the people of Jannah?" Then read the du'a and its simple meaning.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For the youngest, it is enough to give up their turn and say, "This is for Allah." Older children retell Reem's story in their own words.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** The child is never forced to give up a toy; they are encouraged gently and praised when they give. Children are not told unestablished descriptions of the horses of Jannah, such as wings and ruby, and they are not promised a particular toy in Jannah; instead, say: "You will have there whatever you love." Keep safety in mind while riding. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, and the Companions are not drawn.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="8-12" minutes="40" -->

### Children Ages 8-12 — 40 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The student narrates the hadith of the owner of the she-camel and the narration of Ibn Mas'ud; explains the four terms; states an-Nawawi's two possibilities; connects Sami's decision to the hadith; distinguishes what is authentic about the mounts of Jannah from what is not established; and begins their ride ticket.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** The text of the hadith and the two ayat, written out; term cards; the ride-ticket template; two situation cards: "A classmate left a heavy bag behind on the playing field" and "An elderly neighbor is waiting for the bus in the rain."

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher writes on the board: "One she-camel → seven hundred she-camels," reviews the commentaries of an-Nawawi and al-Qurtubi in age-appropriate language, and agrees with parents on the limits of safe lending.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** The teacher asks: "What is the most precious thing you ride or use to get around? Have you ever lent it to anyone? How did that feel?"

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 10 minutes:** Read the ayah of an-Nahl, then the ayah of Fussilat with Ibn Kathir's words, then retell the hadith of the she-camel and Ibn Mas'ud's narration. Explain the *khitam* and the idea of multiplying, and set out an-Nawawi's two possibilities.

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 10 minutes:** Read Sami's story, pausing at the night he hesitated ("What would you have done?") and again at the scratch ("Why didn't he blame his friend?"). Then students work in pairs on the two situation cards, writing for each one a "mount" that could be lent and a first step.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 10 minutes:** Students fill in the "My mount" box and the lending plan on their ticket, as in the activity unit, and agree to show it to their parents.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 5 minutes:** The student completes: "The man gave a she-camel and was promised..." and "What is established about the mounts of Jannah is that..." Then read the du'a. The tickets are collected after five days.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** Students who are struggling get a ready-made list of mounts and needs to choose from. Advanced students write a short dialogue between Sami and Badr on the day the bike was returned.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Mounts are lent only with parents' permission and only to someone who can use them properly, and no student is asked to lend anything that could put them or anyone else in danger. No one is mocked for not owning a mount. The descriptions of the horses of Jannah found in weak reports are not presented to students as established.

### Teens, Ages 13+ — 45 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The teen explains what the ayah of Fussilat, Muslim 1892 and Ibn Mas'ud's narration indicate; presents an-Nawawi's two possibilities and al-Qurtubi's words; defines a weak hadith and a *mursal* hadith; analyzes Nour's decisions to verify before sharing and to give up her lesson; and carries out the activity over seven days.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** A copy of the lesson; private notebooks; the ride-ticket template; a mock screenshot of a "share or else" religious post, with no names; a device connected to a reliable hadith encyclopedia to demonstrate how to search.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher reviews the commentaries of an-Nawawi and al-Qurtubi on Muslim 1892 and the hadith scholars' rulings on at-Tirmidhi 2543, and practices searching for a hadith in a reliable encyclopedia and reading the "summary of the hadith scholar's ruling."

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** Show the screenshot of the post: "What do you do when you get a message like this? And why do messages like this spread so fast?"

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 12 minutes:** Read the ayah of Fussilat with both tafsirs, then retell the hadith of the she-camel and Ibn Mas'ud's narration with the words of an-Nawawi and al-Qurtubi, then the ayah of an-Nahl, and explain the terms. The teacher uses the device to show how to look up the ruling on a hadith.

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 10 minutes:** Read Nour's story, and the groups discuss: "Why was staying silent tempting? What made her correction acceptable? And why didn't her disappointment lessen her longing?" Then: "What connects the correction with giving up the lesson?"

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 12 minutes:** Each student identifies their real mount and writes a lending plan in their notebook, as in the activity unit, without naming anyone.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 6 minutes:** On an unnamed card, each student writes a short, courteous message correcting someone who attributed a weak hadith to the Prophet; then read the du'a. After seven days, anyone who wishes may share the two lines from their ticket.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** Students who are uncomfortable writing may make a private voice recording instead. Advanced students compare Muslim's wording "on the Day of Resurrection" with Ibn Mas'ud's wording "in Jannah," and explain how it bears on an-Nawawi's preference.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Verification is taught with courtesy, not superiority, and no one who shared a mistake may be shamed. Students are not asked to film or post their giving. Make clear that judging a hadith weak does not mean denying the delights of Jannah; it means guarding what is attributed to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him. Private notebooks are not read.

At-Tirmidhi said: "Its chain is not strong," and relayed from al-Bukhari that Abu Sawrah is "*munkar al-hadith*" (his narrations are rejected); al-Albani and Ahmad Shakir graded it weak, as shown in the fawazahmed0 edition of *Jami' at-Tirmidhi* (2544). The hadith of Jabir: "horses of red ruby that have wings," Abu Nu'aym in *Sifat al-Jannah* (429), which turns on al-Hakam ibn Abi Khalid, who is abandoned (*matruk*) and accused of lying, as the editor states. Abu Nu'aym's other reports in the chapter (426-428) have weak chains, as the editor shows. And the *mawquf* statement of 'Abdullah ibn 'Amr, "In Jannah are the finest of horses and the noblest of thoroughbred camels," recorded by Ibn Abi ad-Dunya in *Sifat al-Jannah* (252), has a break in the chain between Qatadah and 'Abdullah ibn 'Amr. The definitions of *mursal* and weak in the terms unit are the lesson's own summary of the hadith scholars' terminology.
