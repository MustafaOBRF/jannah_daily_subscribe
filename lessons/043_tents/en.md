# The Tents and Sheltered Chambers of Jannah

## Lesson Objectives and Outcomes

After this lesson, the learner will be able to:

- Describe the believer's tent in Jannah as the hadith of Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, may Allah be pleased with him, establishes it (al-Bukhari 4879 and 3243, and Muslim 2838): a single hollow pearl, sixty miles wide and sixty miles high into the sky, with a family in each of its corners who cannot see the others.
- Distinguish the tents of Jannah from its palaces and lofty chambers, know that the Qur'an mentions the tents in Allah's words "Hur, kept sheltered in the tents" (ar-Rahman 55:72), and explain briefly and with fitting modesty what "kept sheltered" (*maqsurat*) means according to the commentators.
- Tell how the hadith traveled from Abu Musa to his son Abu Bakr and on to al-Bukhari and Muslim, and how al-Bukhari preserved the narrators' disagreement over "thirty" and "sixty" miles instead of hiding it.
- Connect the traveler's tent in this world with the Prophet's counsel, peace and blessings be upon him: "Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveler passing through" (al-Bukhari 6416), and tell apart the Prophet's own words from the statement of Ibn Umar that follows them.
- Carry out the "Traveler's Light Bag" activity: choosing only what fits in a small load, giving something beyond one's needs to someone who needs it, and keeping up a daily provision of good deeds.
- Recall the lesson's du'a, which asks Allah to make us travelers passing through this world and to settle us in the pearl tents of Jannah.

## Academic Section for Adults

<!-- reader:start audience="adults" estimated_minutes="6.5" -->

<!-- unit:start id="adults.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

In our minds, a tent belongs to travel. It goes up at dusk and comes down at dawn; it is stitched together from many pieces, held fast by stakes and ropes, and shaken by the wind. Revelation tells us that Jannah has tents too, but tents unlike anything in this world: a tent made of a single hollow pearl that rises sixty miles into the sky.

An earlier lesson on the palaces covered the dwellings and homes of Jannah; this lesson pauses at its tents. Ibn al-Qayyim, may Allah have mercy on him, wrote: "These tents are something other than the lofty chambers and the palaces; they are tents in the gardens and on the banks of the rivers."[^9] Allah mentions them in Surah ar-Rahman alongside the description of the hur who are "kept sheltered" (*maqsurat*) within them, and we will treat this with the modesty and brevity it calls for, keeping to what is authentic. The books on the description of Jannah contain many reports about the number of the tents' doors and door-leaves, some of whose chains the editors have shown to be weak, so we build nothing on them.[^9]

We pair this report with another authentic hadith that turns the tent of this world into a key for understanding all of life: "Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveler passing through." The traveler's tent in this world is temporary; the believer's tent in Jannah is a home to stay in.

## Central Evidence

<!-- evidence:start id="bukhari-4879" kind="hadith" mode="canonical" -->

### "A Tent Made of a Hollow Pearl"

> عَنْ أَبِي بَكْرِ بْنِ عَبْدِ اللَّهِ بْنِ قَيْسٍ، عَنْ أَبِيهِ أَبِي مُوسَى الْأَشْعَرِيِّ رضي الله عنه، أَنَّ رَسُولَ اللَّهِ ﷺ قَالَ: **«إِنَّ فِي الْجَنَّةِ خَيْمَةً مِنْ لُؤْلُؤَةٍ مُجَوَّفَةٍ، عَرْضُهَا سِتُّونَ مِيلًا، فِي كُلِّ زَاوِيَةٍ مِنْهَا أَهْلٌ، مَا يَرَوْنَ الْآخَرِينَ، يَطُوفُ عَلَيْهِمُ الْمُؤْمِنُونَ»**. [وَتَتِمَّةُ الرِّوَايَةِ فِي صِفَةِ جَنَّتَيْنِ مِنْ فِضَّةٍ وَجَنَّتَيْنِ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ، وَلَيْسَتْ مِنْ مَوْضُوعِ هَذَا الدَّرْسِ.][^1]
>
> وَفِي رِوَايَةِ مُسْلِمٍ: **«إِنَّ لِلْمُؤْمِنِ فِي الْجَنَّةِ لَخَيْمَةً مِنْ لُؤْلُؤَةٍ وَاحِدَةٍ مُجَوَّفَةٍ، طُولُهَا سِتُّونَ مِيلًا، لِلْمُؤْمِنِ فِيهَا أَهْلُونَ، يَطُوفُ عَلَيْهِمُ الْمُؤْمِنُ، فَلَا يَرَى بَعْضُهُمْ بَعْضًا»**.[^2]
>
> وَفِي رِوَايَةٍ لِلْبُخَارِيِّ: **«الْخَيْمَةُ دُرَّةٌ مُجَوَّفَةٌ، طُولُهَا فِي السَّمَاءِ ثَلَاثُونَ مِيلًا، فِي كُلِّ زَاوِيَةٍ مِنْهَا لِلْمُؤْمِنِ أَهْلٌ لَا يَرَاهُمُ الْآخَرُونَ»**. قَالَ الْبُخَارِيُّ: قَالَ أَبُو عَبْدِ الصَّمَدِ وَالْحَارِثُ بْنُ عُبَيْدٍ عَنْ أَبِي عِمْرَانَ: سِتُّونَ مِيلًا.[^3]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> Abu Bakr ibn Abdullah ibn Qays narrated from his father, Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, may Allah be pleased with him, that the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: **"In Jannah there is a tent made of a hollow pearl, sixty miles wide. In every corner of it there is a family who do not see the others, and the believers go around to visit them."** [The rest of the narration describes two gardens of silver and two gardens of gold, and lies outside the subject of this lesson.][^1]
>
> In the narration of Muslim: **"The believer will have in Jannah a tent made of a single hollow pearl, sixty miles in length. The believer will have families in it, and he will go around to visit them, and they will not see one another."**[^2]
>
> In another narration of al-Bukhari: **"The tent is a hollow pearl, thirty miles high into the sky. In every corner of it the believer has a family whom the others do not see."** Al-Bukhari said: Abu 'Abd al-Samad and al-Harith ibn 'Ubayd said, narrating from Abu 'Imran: "Sixty miles."[^3]

#### Scholarly Explanation

An-Nawawi, may Allah have mercy on him, said: "As for *al-khaymah* (the tent), it is a square dwelling of the kind the Bedouin Arabs live in," and "*al-zawiyah* (the corner) means a side or a quarter." On reconciling "its width" with "its height into the sky," he said: "There is no contradiction between them: its width is the extent of the ground it covers, and its length into the sky is its height, and the two are equal."[^4] "Thirty miles" is the narration of Hammam, and Ibn Hajar explained what al-Bukhari meant by his closing note: "He means that these two narrated this hadith with this same chain and said 'sixty' in place of Hammam's 'thirty.'"[^3] The "family" in the hadith refers to the believer's spouses in Jannah, which is why the chapter in Sahih Muslim carries the title "Chapter on the description of the tents of Jannah and the families the believers will have in them."[^2]

#### Lesson Explanation

The hadith gives three qualities. Its material: a single pearl, not pieces stitched together. Its vastness: sixty miles across and sixty miles high, many times taller than the tallest mountains on earth. And its perfect privacy: each side has its own family, and none of them sees the others. The tent of this world is cramped and open to the wind and to prying eyes; the tent of Jannah is spacious and protected. And in the way al-Bukhari preserved both wordings while naming those who differed, there is a lesson in trustworthiness: he did not hide the disagreement; he passed it on exactly as he had heard it.

<!-- evidence:end -->

<!-- evidence:start id="quran-55-70-72" kind="quran" mode="canonical" -->

### "Hur, Kept Sheltered in the Tents"

> **{فِيهِنَّ خَيْرَاتٌ حِسَانٌ ۝ فَبِأَيِّ آلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ ۝ حُورٌ مَقْصُورَاتٌ فِي الْخِيَامِ}** [الرحمن: ٧٠-٧٢][^5]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> **"In them are maidens good and beautiful. So which of the favors of your Lord will you both deny? Hur, kept sheltered in the tents."** (ar-Rahman 55:70-72)[^5]

#### Scholarly Explanation

At-Tabari records two views on "kept sheltered" (*maqsurat*): that they "are kept for their husbands alone and seek no one in their place," and that the meaning is "they are kept within their canopied chambers," that is, guarded in their private quarters.[^6] Ibn Kathir, after comparing the ayah with Allah's words "In them are those who restrain their glances" (ar-Rahman 55:56), said: "even though all of them are kept in seclusion," and then cited the hadith of the tent made of a hollow pearl in explaining the words "in the tents."[^7] As-Sa'di said: "Kept within tents of pearl... and this does not rule out their going out into the gardens and meadows of Jannah."[^8] It is in this sense of protection and privacy that Ibn al-Qayyim titled one of his chapters "On its lofty chambers, its palaces, its sheltered chambers, and its tents."[^9]

#### Lesson Explanation

The ayah describes the bliss of Jannah through protection, not display: those who dwell in the tents are honored and veiled, and their beauty is not cheapened by being laid open to every eye. This teaches us that modesty and privacy are marks of perfection in this world and the next. In teaching, we stop at this modest measure and leave the details to what the text itself says.

<!-- evidence:end -->

<!-- evidence:start id="bukhari-6416" kind="hadith" mode="canonical" -->

### "Be in This World as Though You Were a Stranger or a Traveler Passing Through"

> عَنْ عَبْدِ اللَّهِ بْنِ عُمَرَ رضي الله عنهما قَالَ: أَخَذَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ بِمَنْكِبِي فَقَالَ: **«كُنْ فِي الدُّنْيَا كَأَنَّكَ غَرِيبٌ، أَوْ عَابِرُ سَبِيلٍ»**. وَكَانَ ابْنُ عُمَرَ يَقُولُ: إِذَا أَمْسَيْتَ فَلَا تَنْتَظِرِ الصَّبَاحَ، وَإِذَا أَصْبَحْتَ فَلَا تَنْتَظِرِ الْمَسَاءَ، وَخُذْ مِنْ صِحَّتِكَ لِمَرَضِكَ، وَمِنْ حَيَاتِكَ لِمَوْتِكَ.[^10]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> Abdullah ibn Umar, may Allah be pleased with them both, said: The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, took hold of my shoulder and said: **"Be in this world as though you were a stranger, or a traveler passing through."** And Ibn Umar used to say: "When evening comes, do not wait for the morning, and when morning comes, do not wait for the evening. Take from your health for your sickness, and from your life for your death."[^10]

#### Scholarly Explanation

The part of the hadith that comes from the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, is the counsel itself: "Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveler passing through." What follows is a statement of Ibn Umar, may Allah be pleased with them both, his own words (*mawquf*), expressing what he understood from the counsel and how he lived by it.[^10]

#### Lesson Explanation

The hadith does not mention tents, yet the image of the passing traveler meets them on the road. A traveler does not build a palace along the way; he pitches a light tent, carries a little useful provision, and keeps his heart fixed on where he is going. Whoever lives in this spirit does not neglect the world, but neither does he make it his home, and he hopes to settle there, in a tent that is never folded away.

<!-- evidence:end -->

### Activity: The Traveler's Light Bag

<!-- activity:start audience="adults" concept_id="lesson.043.activity.travelers-light-bag" -->

Choose one drawer, shelf, or closet in your home. Imagine you are leaving on a journey tomorrow and can take only one small bag. Set aside what you would take, and sort the rest into two piles: "I need this at home" and "More than I need." From the second pile, pick one thing that is still in good, usable condition, and within seven days get it to a person or an organization that needs it. Then choose one daily "provision" for the Hereafter (a portion of Qur'an, a small act of charity, or reaching out to a relative) and keep it up for the same seven days. At the end of the week, write a paragraph: What was hardest to give away? And how did the counsel to be "a traveler passing through" change the way you look at what you own?

### An Authentic Hadith: A Tent Made of One Pearl

<!-- story:start audience="4-7" role="primary" type="hadith" source_id="bukhari-4879" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true report from Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim. It is not a made-up story.**

<!-- retelling:start source_id="bukhari-4879" audience="4-7" -->

Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, may Allah be pleased with him, was one of the Companions of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him. He heard the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, say that the believer will have a tent in Jannah made of one single pearl.[^2]

Just one pearl! Have you ever seen a little white pearl, shining and shimmering? The tent in Jannah isn't lots of pieces of cloth sewn together. It is one big pearl. And it is hollow, which means there is lots and lots of room inside.[^4]

And how tall is it? Sixty miles! It reaches up into the sky, much, much higher than the highest mountain in the world, and it is just as wide as it is tall.[^1][^4] In every side of it, the believer has family, and it is so big that they can't even see one another.[^3]

Abu Musa remembered this, and then he told it to his son, Abu Bakr. His son told it to someone else, who told someone else, until Imam al-Bukhari and Imam Muslim wrote it down in their books.[^1][^2] And that is how it has come all the way to us today.

Look what happened: a father taught his son something beautiful about Jannah, and it reached people everywhere. You can tell it to your brother or sister tonight, too. And remember: we fold up a cloth tent in the morning, but the pearl tent belongs to the believer in Jannah, and it will never, ever be folded up.

### An Authentic Hadith: The Believer's Tent in Jannah

<!-- story:start audience="8-12" role="primary" type="hadith" source_id="bukhari-4879" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true report narrated by al-Bukhari and Muslim. It is not a made-up story.**

<!-- retelling:start source_id="bukhari-4879" audience="8-12" -->

Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, may Allah be pleased with him, was one of the Companions who memorized the words of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, and passed them on. One of the things he heard him say was: "The believer will have in Jannah a tent made of a single hollow pearl, sixty miles in length."[^2]

Stop and look at each word. "A tent": to the Arabs, a tent was a square dwelling of the kind the Bedouin lived in.[^4] "Made of a single pearl": not pieces sewn together, but one single jewel. "Hollow": with a vast space inside where one can live. "Sixty miles": one narration says the tent is sixty miles wide, and another says it is sixty miles high into the sky, and the scholars explained that both are true: its width across the ground is the same as its height into the sky.[^1][^4] Imagine it: it is many times taller than the tallest mountain on earth.

The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, also said that in every corner of it the believer has a family who do not see the others, and the believer visits them.[^1] So it is enormous, and every part of it is private and peaceful.

Then the journey of the hadith itself began. Abu Musa told it to his son, Abu Bakr, and Abu Bakr told it to a man named Abu 'Imran al-Jawni. From there it passed from one careful memorizer to the next, until Imam al-Bukhari and Imam Muslim wrote it down in their two books of authentic hadith.[^1][^2] A father passes a true word on to his son, and hundreds of years later it reaches you.

Now compare: a camping tent gets folded up and carried off, but the tent of Jannah is the believer's permanent home. That is why a Muslim lives in this world like a traveler: he works and tries his best, but he doesn't weigh down his bag with things he doesn't need, and he gathers his provision from obeying Allah.

## For Teens, Ages 13+

<!-- reader:start audience="13+" estimated_minutes="5.5" -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

We live in a culture that tells us every single day: buy more, collect more, show more. A newer phone, a nicer room, photos posted for everyone to see. Into the middle of all this comes a short piece of Prophetic advice: "Be in this world as though you were a stranger or a traveler passing through."[^10] A traveler passing through doesn't hate the road, but he doesn't build a palace on it either. He pitches a light tent and moves on. And at the end of the road there is a tent of a very different kind. Allah mentioned it in Surah ar-Rahman, and the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, described it in a way worth pausing over, not only for what it says about the tent, but also for the journey the description took to reach us.

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.terms" kind="terms" -->

<!-- terminology:start source_id="bukhari-4879" -->

- **`Hollow (mujawwafah)`** — empty on the inside, with a vast space within where one can live; one narration reads *mujawwabah*, "pierced through," which carries the same meaning.[^4]

<!-- terminology:end -->

<!-- terminology:start source_id="quran-55-70-72" -->

- **`Kept sheltered (maqsurat)`** — guarded and protected within the tents, or keeping their gaze for their husbands alone; both meanings come down to protection and modesty.[^6][^7]

<!-- terminology:end -->

<!-- unit:end -->

### An Authentic Hadith: A Tent Made of a Pearl, and Narrators Who Hid No Number

<!-- story:start audience="13+" role="primary" type="hadith" source_id="bukhari-4879" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true report narrated by al-Bukhari and Muslim. It is not a made-up story.**

<!-- retelling:start source_id="bukhari-4879" audience="13+" -->

The speaker is the Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, and the listener is Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, may Allah be pleased with him. The report: "In Jannah there is a tent made of a hollow pearl, sixty miles wide. In every corner of it there is a family who do not see the others, and the believers go around to visit them."[^1] In Muslim's wording it is "a single pearl," and "the believer will have families in it."[^2] No stitching, no stakes, no cloth snapping in the wind: just one jewel, rising into the sky many times higher than the tallest mountains on earth, with every side of it screened from the others.[^4] Ibn Kathir cited this hadith in explaining Allah's words "Hur, kept sheltered in the tents,"[^7] so beauty in Jannah is protected, not put on display for every eye.[^5]

Then the second story begins: the journey of the hadith. Abu Musa tells it to his son, Abu Bakr; Abu Bakr tells it to Abu 'Imran al-Jawni; and from Abu 'Imran a number of narrators pass it on.[^1][^3] And here a small problem shows up. One narrator, named Hammam, said: "thirty miles high into the sky," while other narrators said: "sixty miles."

So what did Imam al-Bukhari do? He could have picked one number and quietly dropped the other, and nobody would have noticed. Instead, he recorded Hammam's wording exactly as it was, and right after it he wrote: Abu 'Abd al-Samad and al-Harith ibn 'Ubayd said, narrating from Abu 'Imran: "Sixty miles."[^3] And he recorded the other narration elsewhere in his book.[^1] Later, Ibn Hajar came along and explained this, showing that these two had narrated the hadith with the very same chain and said "sixty" instead of "thirty."[^3] No one hid anything, and that is exactly why we can trust what has reached us today.

The lesson for you comes in two parts. First, living like a traveler passing through doesn't mean neglecting your studies or your future. It means not letting what you own, or what people see of you, become the measure of your worth. Your real home is a tent that is never folded away. Second, being trustworthy in passing on what you hear, even down to a small number, is part of your religion. When you pass on something a friend said, or share a post, pass it on as it is, and don't cut out the parts you don't like.

<!-- retelling:end -->

### Discussion Questions

1. Why is it striking that the tent of Jannah is "a single pearl," compared with the tents of this world?
2. What did al-Bukhari do when the narrators disagreed about the number, and why does that make us trust the hadith more?
3. What is the difference between living as "a traveler passing through" and neglecting this world?
4. How does the description "kept sheltered" (*maqsurat*) push back against a culture of "show everything"?
5. What do you own, or spend your time on, that wouldn't fit in a traveler's load?

<!-- unit:end -->

### Activity: The Traveler's Light Bag

<!-- activity:start audience="13+" concept_id="lesson.043.activity.travelers-light-bag" -->

On a private sheet of paper, list ten things you own or spend time on every day (belongings, apps, or subscriptions). Ask of each one: "Would this fit in a light traveler's load?" and mark it "Need it," "Extra," or "Weighs me down." Choose one "Extra" item and give it to someone who can use it, and choose one "Weighs me down" item and step away from it for seven days. In its place, choose a daily provision for the Hereafter to keep up over the same seven days, and track it privately without posting about it. At the end of the week, write a paragraph: How did it feel when the bag got lighter? And what did you learn about your worth apart from what you own?

### Bedtime Du'a

<!-- bedtime-dua:start audience="13+" id="lesson.043.dua.travelers-provision-pearl-tents" provenance="lesson_authored" source_id="" attribution="not_prophetic" -->

**Lesson-authored thematic du'a; not attributed to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him.**

> اللَّهُمَّ اجْعَلْنَا فِي الدُّنْيَا كَعَابِرِي سَبِيلٍ، وَاجْعَلْ زَادَنَا طَاعَتَكَ، وَأَنْزِلْنَا فِي جَنَّتِكَ خِيَامَ اللُّؤْلُؤِ آمِنِينَ.
>
> *"O Allah, make us like travelers passing through this world, make obeying You our provision, and settle us, safe and secure, in the pearl tents of Your Jannah."*

What it means: O Allah, make us travelers in this world who are not held captive by things or by people's gazes. Make obeying You our provision along the way, and at its end settle us in the pearl tents of Your Jannah, safe and at peace.

## Detailed Teaching Plans

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="adults" minutes="60" -->

### Adults — 60 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The learner names the three qualities of the tent of Jannah from the narrations of Abu Musa's hadith, explains an-Nawawi's reconciliation of width and height, and describes how al-Bukhari passed on the disagreement between "thirty" and "sixty"; sets out briefly the two views on "kept sheltered" (*maqsurat*); distinguishes the Prophet's words from Ibn Umar's own statement in the hadith of Ibn Umar; and gives something beyond their needs to someone who needs it, keeping up a daily provision for a week.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Copies of the lesson; a mushaf; a board with three columns: "What is established about the tents," "Trustworthy transmission," and "The traveler's way"; a picture of an ordinary travel tent with no lettering; a pearl or a white bead; the three-part activity sheet; an exit card.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher reads al-Bukhari 3243, 4878, and 4879 and Muslim 2838 in its three routes; an-Nawawi's commentary on Muslim; Ibn Hajar's comments on 3243; the tafsir of at-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, and as-Sa'di on ar-Rahman 55:72; and chapters 36 and 51 of *Hadi al-Arwah* with the editor's notes. The teacher should note that the reports about the number of doors and door-leaves (such as the report from Ibn Mas'ud by way of Jabir al-Ju'fi, and the report from Ibn Abbas about four thousand door-leaves) have chains the editor judged weak, so they are not used. The teacher should also bear in mind that the lesson on the palaces came earlier and should not repeat its material.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** Show the picture of the tent and the pearl and ask: "What makes a tent different from a house? And when do we live in tents in this world?" Write the answers on the board (travel, Hajj, desert life), then ask: "So what does it mean that there are tents in Jannah?"

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 20 minutes:** Read the three narrations of Abu Musa's hadith and ask learners to pick out the differences between them (6 minutes); then read the words of an-Nawawi and Ibn Hajar and fill in the first two columns (6 minutes). Next, read ar-Rahman 55:70-72 and the commentators' views briefly (4 minutes), then the hadith of Ibn Umar, pointing out where the Prophet's words end (4 minutes).

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 10 minutes:** Discuss questions 3 and 6. Steer the discussion toward two points: al-Bukhari's trustworthiness in passing on a single number is a model for the Muslim's trustworthiness in passing on news, and the counsel to be a traveler passing through does not mean giving up work and earning a living, but putting the heart in order: this world is the road, and Jannah is the home. Fill in the third column.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 15 minutes:** Each learner decides which space they will sort through (3 minutes), writes down what they expect will turn out to be "more than I need" and whom they will give it to (5 minutes), and chooses their daily provision; then pairs share the obstacle they expect and how they will get past it (7 minutes).

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 10 minutes:** Exit card: "Name two established qualities of the tent of Jannah, one lesson from the way the hadith was transmitted, and the thing you will give away this week." Then read the hadith of Ibn Umar one last time, and agree to review the activity in a week.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For beginners, Muslim's narration and the hadith of Ibn Umar are enough. Ask advanced learners to gather the routes of the hadith in the two Sahihs and compare their wordings, and to review the note by the editor of *Hadi al-Arwah* on preferring "sixty" over "thirty."

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Mention the hur and the believer's family briefly and modestly, without detail or imagery beyond the text. Do not present any conversion of the mile into kilometers as if it came from the text. Do not use the weak reports about the number of doors, or defective narrations, as evidence. Point out that detachment from the world does not mean neglecting what one owes one's family and oneself, and that what is given away should be something the learner owns and that is still usable, without embarrassing anyone who has little.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="4-7" minutes="25" -->

### Children Ages 4-7 — 25 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The child says that the believer will have a tent in Jannah made of one single hollow pearl that is very, very big; tells how Abu Musa passed this report on to his son; chooses only three things for the bag; and gives a toy they don't need to another child.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** A blanket and two chairs; eight toys or small objects; a small bag; a shiny white pearl or bead; large sheets of paper and crayons; the du'a card.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The caregiver sets up a spot for the tent and agrees with the family ahead of time on a toy suitable for giving away and on who will receive it. The caregiver also practices telling the report without adding anything to the description of the tent beyond what has come in the text.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 4 minutes:** The caregiver sits with the child under the blanket and asks: "Is this a strong tent? What would happen if the wind blew?" Then shows the pearl: "Imagine a tent made of one big pearl!"

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Reading the Evidence — 7 minutes:** The caregiver reads the explanation and then the report, pausing at the word "hollow" to show the child an empty cup as an example, then repeats together with the child: "A tent made of one pearl."

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 4 minutes:** Ask questions 1, 3, and 4, and stress that Abu Musa, a father, taught his son, and that the child can teach a brother or sister too.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 7 minutes:** The child chooses three things for the bag and takes them into the tent, then chooses the toy they will give away together with their dad or mom, and starts drawing the pearl tent.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 3 minutes:** Notice whether the child can say what the tent of Jannah is made of, and whether they chose only three things. Then read the du'a together.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For the youngest, the single sentence "The believer will have a tent in Jannah made of a pearl" and choosing two things are enough. Ask older children to tell the report to a brother or sister in three sentences.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not draw the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, or the Companions. Do not force the child to give away a toy they are attached to; let them choose one they are happy to give. Do not leave the child alone inside the homemade tent, and do not use coverings that could make breathing difficult. Do not add anything to the description of Jannah as if it were certain when it has not come in the texts.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="8-12" minutes="40" -->

### Children Ages 8-12 — 40 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The student retells the hadith with its three qualities and the scholars' reconciliation of width and height; draws the chain by which the hadith traveled from Abu Musa to al-Bukhari and Muslim; explains the meaning of "a traveler passing through"; and gives away something extra, keeping up a daily provision for five days.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Term cards; a small backpack; a trip-list sheet; a five-day chart; paper for drawing the chain of narrators; a picture of a camping tent with no lettering.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher writes the chain of narrators on separate cards (the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him; Abu Musa; his son Abu Bakr; Abu 'Imran al-Jawni; al-Bukhari and Muslim) for students to put in order. The teacher also contacts families about the giving so that it happens with their permission.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** Ask: "Who has slept in a tent? What's the hardest thing about sleeping in one?" Then ask: "Why doesn't a traveler build a house along the road?"

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 12 minutes:** Read the explanation and the hadith of Ibn Umar (3 minutes), then the story, pausing at each word of the tent's description (5 minutes); then students put the narrator cards in order and draw the chain (4 minutes).

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 8 minutes:** Ask questions 2, 4, and 5. Make clear that a traveler passing through works hard at school and at work, but does not weigh himself down with what he doesn't need, and keeps the destination in mind.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 10 minutes:** Each student writes the trip list and crosses things off until they fit in the backpack, sorts what was crossed off, then chooses a daily provision and sets up the five-day chart.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 5 minutes:** Each student writes: "Two qualities of the tent of Jannah, the names of two narrators in the chain of the hadith, and the provision I chose." Then read the du'a.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For students who struggle, provide a ready-made trip list for them to cross things off. Ask advanced students to memorize the hadith of Ibn Umar and to research the "thirty miles" narration and who narrated it.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not ask students to show what they gave away in front of their classmates, out of consideration for those who don't have much. Giving happens with the parents' permission. Do not draw the Companions or act out their roles. Treat the mention of "family" in the hadith in its general sense, without going into detail.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="13+" minutes="50" -->

### Teens, Ages 13+ — 50 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The teen describes the tent of Jannah from the narrations of the hadith and analyzes al-Bukhari's handling of the narrators' disagreement as a model of trustworthiness; explains the meaning of "kept sheltered" (*maqsurat*) briefly and connects it to the value of privacy and modesty; distinguishes the spirit of the traveler passing through from neglecting this world; and carries out the ten-item inventory for a week alongside a private daily provision.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Copies of the lesson; a private sheet that is not collected; the text of Hammam's narration and al-Bukhari's note written on the board; cards for anonymous questions; a mushaf.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher prepares two neutral examples of a piece of news passed on with part of it cut out (a sports story, or a friend's comment passed on incompletely), and gets ready for the question: "Does detachment from the world mean I shouldn't own a phone?"

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** Ask: "If you had to live for a month in a tent with one bag, what would you take?" Listen without commenting, then read the counsel to be a traveler passing through.

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 12 minutes:** Read the story, pausing at three moments: the description of the tent, the appearance of the disagreement over the number, and al-Bukhari's response. Then read the terms and ar-Rahman 55:72 briefly.

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 13 minutes:** Discuss the two examples in groups in light of question 2, then questions 3 and 4. The teacher guides students toward the idea that privacy and modesty are a dignity, not a deprivation, and that a person's worth does not lie in what they own or display.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 12 minutes:** Each student writes their ten-item list on their private sheet and sorts it, decides what they will give away and what they will step away from, and chooses their daily provision.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 8 minutes:** Each student writes a paragraph: "What did I learn from al-Bukhari's way of passing on a single number?" Then read the du'a. A week later, the teacher asks a general question about the experience without asking for details.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** Students who find writing difficult may make a private audio recording instead. Ask advanced students to do a short research piece on the three routes of the hadith in Sahih Muslim and compare their wordings.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** No one is asked to reveal their list or what they own. Treat the meaning of "kept sheltered" (*maqsurat*) briefly and modestly, without going into the description of the hur. Point out that stepping away from an app or a habit must not come at the expense of a school obligation or necessary contact with family, and that anyone who feels addicted or distressed should talk to a trusted adult.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->
