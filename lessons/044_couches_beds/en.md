# The Couches, Canopied Seats and Furnishings of Jannah

## Lesson Objectives and Outcomes

After this lesson, the learner will be able to:

- Narrate the Qur'anic scene in Surah al-Mutaffifin (83:22-36): people who used to laugh at the believers, wink at one another about them and accuse them of being astray, and then a Day on which those same believers are "on canopied couches, gazing."
- Explain the meanings of *surur* (couches), *ara'ik* (canopied couches), *namariq* (cushions) and *zarabi* (carpets) from the statements of the commentators: the *ara'ik* are couches set within curtained canopies, *mawdunah* means woven with gold, the *namariq* are cushions and the *zarabi* are carpets, while keeping clear what is in the text and what is commentary.
- Show what "facing one another" says about purity of heart and refined manners, and what "well pleased with their striving" says about this rest being the fruit of effort made in this world.
- Distinguish Allah's just recompense for the mockers on the Day of Resurrection from the believer's conduct in this world: he does not mock, and he does not appoint himself a watchman over people, as the ayah says: "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them."
- Carry out the "I Won't Laugh, I'll Redirect, I'll Sit Beside Them" activity: a three-step plan put into practice when real mockery happens, with a weekly log and a reflection.
- Learn by heart the Prophet's du'a, peace and blessings be upon him, for lying down in bed: "In Your name, my Lord, I lay down my side..." (al-Bukhari 6320).

## Academic Section for Adults

<!-- reader:start audience="adults" estimated_minutes="6.5" -->

<!-- unit:start id="adults.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

In human life, where you sit is a sign of where you stand: who gets the seat of honor, who has room made for them, who is left standing by the door. Laughter itself can become a way of handing out the seats. Some people rise by mocking others, and others are pushed to the margins because they prayed, wore hijab or refused to take part in something wrong.

When the Qur'an describes Jannah, it mentions couches, canopied seats, cushions and carpets. It does not mention them as mere furniture, but as seats of honor, rest and serenity. This lesson reads the ayat of Surah al-Mutaffifin that make the "canopied couches" Allah's answer to the gatherings of mockery in this world, then sets beside them the ayat of al-Ghashiyah and al-Waqi'ah that describe those seats, and then asks: how do we sit today, and whom do we laugh with?

## Central Evidence

<!-- evidence:start id="quran-83-22-36" kind="quran" mode="canonical" -->

### "On Canopied Couches, Gazing"

> **{إِنَّ الْأَبْرَارَ لَفِي نَعِيمٍ ۝ عَلَى الْأَرَائِكِ يَنظُرُونَ ۝ تَعْرِفُ فِي وُجُوهِهِمْ نَضْرَةَ النَّعِيمِ ۝ يُسْقَوْنَ مِن رَّحِيقٍ مَّخْتُومٍ ۝ خِتَامُهُ مِسْكٌ ۚ وَفِي ذَٰلِكَ فَلْيَتَنَافَسِ الْمُتَنَافِسُونَ ۝ وَمِزَاجُهُ مِن تَسْنِيمٍ ۝ عَيْنًا يَشْرَبُ بِهَا الْمُقَرَّبُونَ ۝ إِنَّ الَّذِينَ أَجْرَمُوا كَانُوا مِنَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا يَضْحَكُونَ ۝ وَإِذَا مَرُّوا بِهِمْ يَتَغَامَزُونَ ۝ وَإِذَا انقَلَبُوا إِلَىٰ أَهْلِهِمُ انقَلَبُوا فَكِهِينَ ۝ وَإِذَا رَأَوْهُمْ قَالُوا إِنَّ هَٰؤُلَاءِ لَضَالُّونَ ۝ وَمَا أُرْسِلُوا عَلَيْهِمْ حَافِظِينَ ۝ فَالْيَوْمَ الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا مِنَ الْكُفَّارِ يَضْحَكُونَ ۝ عَلَى الْأَرَائِكِ يَنظُرُونَ ۝ هَلْ ثُوِّبَ الْكُفَّارُ مَا كَانُوا يَفْعَلُونَ}** [المطففين: ٢٢-٣٦][^1]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> **"The truly righteous will be in bliss, on canopied couches, gazing. You will recognize in their faces the radiance of bliss. They will be given to drink a pure, sealed wine, whose seal is musk; so for this let all who strive, strive. Its mixture is from Tasnim, a spring from which those brought near will drink. The criminals used to laugh at those who believed; when they passed by them, they would wink at one another; when they went back to their own people, they went back relishing it; and when they saw them, they would say, 'These people have surely gone astray!' Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them. So today it is those who believed who laugh at the disbelievers, on canopied couches, gazing. Have the disbelievers not been repaid for what they used to do?"** (al-Mutaffifin 83:22-36)[^1]

#### Scholarly Explanation

On "on canopied couches" (*'ala al-ara'ik*), Ibn Kathir says: "They are couches beneath canopies." On "gazing" he gives two views: they gaze at their kingdom and at the unending good Allah has given them, or they gaze at Allah, Mighty and Majestic, in contrast to what is said of the wicked, that they will be veiled from their Lord. At-Tabari reports from Ibn Abbas: "The *ara'ik* are couches within curtained canopies," and from Mujahid that they are made of pearl and ruby.[^1][^2] As-Sa'di brings the two meanings together: on couches adorned with fine furnishings, they gaze at the bliss Allah has prepared for them, and they gaze at the noble Face of their Lord.[^3]

On ayat 29-32, Ibn Kathir says that in this world they used to laugh at the believers, "that is, mock them and look down on them," and wink at one another about them when they passed by them; and when they returned to their homes, they returned relishing it. "And for all that, they did not thank Allah for His favor upon them; rather, they busied themselves with the believers, belittling them and envying them." On "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them," he says they had not been sent as watchmen over the deeds and words of the believers, nor charged with them, "so why did they busy themselves with them and keep them always in their sights?" He then cites the ayat of al-Mu'minun (23:108-111), which state that Allah rewarded those who were mocked "for their patience."[^1] And on "Have they been repaid," as-Sa'di says they were recompensed in kind for what they did, "as justice and wisdom from Allah."[^3]

#### Lesson Explanation

The passage opens with the canopied couches and closes with them: at the start they describe the righteous, and at the end they are an answer to the scene of mockery. So the high seat in Jannah is not mentioned here as a luxury standing on its own, but as the restoration of dignity to people who were looked down on in this world because of their faith. And the Qur'an attributes this answer to Allah on the Day of Resurrection, not to the believer in this world; it gives the believer no license to repay mockery with mockery today.

<!-- evidence:end -->

### "Raised Couches and Cushions Lined in Rows"

> **{وُجُوهٌ يَوْمَئِذٍ نَّاعِمَةٌ ۝ لِّسَعْيِهَا رَاضِيَةٌ ۝ فِي جَنَّةٍ عَالِيَةٍ ۝ لَّا تَسْمَعُ فِيهَا لَاغِيَةً ۝ فِيهَا عَيْنٌ جَارِيَةٌ ۝ فِيهَا سُرُرٌ مَّرْفُوعَةٌ ۝ وَأَكْوَابٌ مَّوْضُوعَةٌ ۝ وَنَمَارِقُ مَصْفُوفَةٌ ۝ وَزَرَابِيُّ مَبْثُوثَةٌ}** [الغاشية: ٨-١٦][^4]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> **"Other faces that Day will glow with bliss, well pleased with their striving, in a lofty garden, where they will hear no idle talk. In it is a flowing spring. In it are raised couches, and cups set out ready, and cushions lined in rows, and carpets spread all around."** (al-Ghashiyah 88:8-16)[^4]

#### Scholarly Explanation

Ibn Kathir says that "glow with bliss" (*na'imah*) means that bliss can be seen in them, "and they attained that only through their striving," and he reports from Sufyan: they are pleased with their deeds. He explains the raised couches as "high, soft, heaped with furnishings, lofty in their height," and reports from Ibn Abbas: "The *namariq* are cushions," and "the *zarabi* are carpets"; and "spread all around" (*mabthuthah*) means here and there, for whoever wishes to sit on them.[^4] As-Sa'di says the couches are "seats raised high in themselves and by the soft, plush furnishings upon them"; the cushions have been lined up for sitting and reclining, "and they have been spared the trouble of setting them out and arranging them themselves"; and "their striving" means the righteous deeds and kindness to Allah's servants that they sent ahead in this world.[^5]

#### Lesson Explanation

The glowing faces are pleased with their "striving": the rest on the raised couches is the fruit of earlier toil, not a substitute for it. And part of the honor is that the seat is ready before its owner arrives.

<!-- evidence:end -->

<!-- evidence:start id="quran-56-15-16" kind="quran" mode="canonical" -->

### "On Couches Woven with Gold"

> **{عَلَىٰ سُرُرٍ مَّوْضُونَةٍ ۝ مُّتَّكِئِينَ عَلَيْهَا مُتَقَابِلِينَ}** [الواقعة: ١٥-١٦][^6]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> **"On couches woven with gold, reclining on them, facing one another."** (al-Waqi'ah 56:15-16)[^6]

#### Scholarly Explanation

Ibn Kathir reports from Ibn Abbas that *mawdunah* means "laced with gold, that is, woven with it," and that Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, Qatadah and others said the same. On "facing one another" (*mutaqabilin*) he says: "Their faces turned toward one another; no one is behind anyone."[^6] At-Tabari gives the root meaning of the word: something woven with its parts worked into one another, the way the rings of a coat of mail are linked.[^2] As-Sa'di says that "reclining" (*muttaki'in*) is the sitting of someone settled, at ease and at rest, and that "facing one another" comes "from the purity of their hearts, the beauty of their manners, and their hearts being turned toward one another."[^6]

#### Lesson Explanation

In the gatherings of this world, someone may end up "behind" the others: backs are turned on him, and winks pass over his head. The gatherings of Jannah are faces turned toward one another, where no one is left behind anyone.

<!-- evidence:end -->

### How Do These Texts Fit Together?

Al-Mutaffifin describes the heart of the gathering: a gathering of mockery in this world, then canopied couches and gazing in the Hereafter. Al-Ghashiyah describes its details: high couches, cushions lined in rows and carpets spread all around, for those who are pleased with their striving. Al-Waqi'ah describes its spirit: faces turned toward one another and hearts made pure. So the practical question is this: are our gatherings today closer to the gathering of those who wink at others, or to the gathering of those who face one another?

## Questions for Understanding and Reflection

1. Why does "on canopied couches, gazing" appear at both the beginning and the end of the passage?
2. What are the two views on the meaning of "gazing," and how did as-Sa'di bring them together?
3. What does "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them" teach us about what drives the mockery of religious people?
4. What is the difference between Allah's recompense for the mockers on the Day of Resurrection and repaying mockery with mockery in this world?
5. When were you last in a gathering where people laughed at someone, absent or present, and what did you do?

### Activity: I Won't Laugh, I'll Redirect, I'll Sit Beside Them

<!-- activity:start audience="adults" concept_id="lesson.044.activity.dont-laugh-redirect-sit-beside" -->

Identify a gathering where laughing at people keeps happening: a work team, a family group chat or a regular get-together with friends. Write out a three-step plan in advance:

- I will not join in the laughter or the mocking emojis.
- A short redirecting line I have prepared, such as: "Let's drop that. What do you all think about...?"
- I will move closer to the person being mocked: sit beside them, or message them privately with a kind word.

Follow the plan for seven days and log every incident: what happened, which step you used and what effect it had. Then write a paragraph: what changed in the gathering, and what changed in you?

### A Story from the Qur'an: Who Sits on the Special Couches?

<!-- story:start audience="4-7" role="primary" type="quranic" source_id="quran-83-22-36" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true story from the Noble Qur'an. It is not a made-up story.**

In Surah al-Mutaffifin, Allah tells us about some people who believed in Him and loved Him. They prayed, and they did good things.

<!-- retelling:start source_id="quran-83-22-36" audience="4-7" -->

But there were some other people who laughed at them. When they walked past the believers, they winked at each other to make fun of them. When they went back home, they were happy about what they had done. And they used to say about the believers, "These people are lost!" That means lost from the right way.[^1]

But Allah sees everything. On the Day of Judgment, the believers who were laughed at will sit on the special couches, happy and comfortable, with their faces shining with joy, looking at all the good things Allah has given them. Nobody will ever make fun of them again. And Allah will give the people who mocked them what their actions earned.[^1][^3]

<!-- retelling:end -->

The believers didn't stop doing good just because someone laughed at them, so Allah honored them with the most beautiful seat of all. And we don't laugh at anyone. We go and sit beside them.

## For Children Ages 8 to 12

<!-- reader:start audience="8-12" estimated_minutes="5.0" -->

<!-- unit:start id="8-12.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

At recess, Yusuf saw the new kid in his class spread a small cloth in a corner of the playground to pray Dhuhr. Two boys laughed, and one winked at the other and said, "Look, it's the little sheikh!" Everyone laughed. Yusuf looked at them, then at his classmate, and he didn't know what to do. Should he laugh along so he wouldn't seem different, or do something else?

In this lesson we read ayat from Surah al-Mutaffifin that describe almost exactly this scene: people who laugh at the believers and wink at each other, and then a Day when those believers sit "on canopied couches, gazing."[^1] We'll also discover the couches, cushions and carpets of Jannah as the Qur'an describes them.[^4][^6]

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="8-12.terms" kind="terms" -->

<!-- terminology:start source_id="quran-83-22-36" -->

- **`Canopied couches (al-ara'ik)`** — the plural of *arikah*: a couch set inside a *hajalah*, which means it has a curtain and a canopy over it, as at-Tabari reports from Ibn Abbas.[^2]

- **`Winking at one another (yataghamazun)`** — signaling to each other with their eyes when they passed the believers; Ibn Kathir says they did it "looking down on them."[^1]

- **`Cushions (namariq)`** — pillows to lean on, as Ibn Abbas said; and **`zarabi`**: carpets.[^4]

- **`Woven with gold (mawdunah)`** — woven through with gold, as Ibn Kathir reports from Ibn Abbas.[^6]

<!-- terminology:end -->

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="8-12.primary-story" kind="primary_story" -->

### A Story from the Qur'an: Two Gatherings

<!-- story:start audience="8-12" role="primary" type="quranic" source_id="quran-83-22-36" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true story from the Noble Qur'an, and the explanations in it come from the commentators of the Qur'an.**

The ayat begin with a promise: "The truly righteous will be in bliss, on canopied couches, gazing." They tell us that if you looked at them, you would recognize in their faces the radiance of bliss, its glow and its joy.[^1][^3]

<!-- retelling:start source_id="quran-83-22-36" audience="8-12" -->

Then the ayat take us back to this world, to the first gathering: "The criminals used to laugh at those who believed." Ibn Kathir says they used to mock them and look down on them. When they passed by them, they winked at each other. When they went home to their families, they went home pleased with what they had done. And when they saw them, they said, "These people have surely gone astray!"[^1]

Then Allah says: "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them." In other words, Allah never put them in charge of watching the believers and judging them. So why were they so busy with them?[^1]

Then comes the second gathering: "So today it is those who believed who laugh at the disbelievers, on canopied couches, gazing." As-Sa'di says the mockers were paid back with the same kind of thing they had done, "as justice and wisdom from Allah," while the believers sit on beautifully adorned couches, gazing at what Allah has prepared for them.[^3]

<!-- retelling:end -->

Notice who turned the two gatherings upside down: it was Allah, on the Day of Resurrection, not the believers in this world. A believer doesn't answer mockery with more mockery. He holds firm to doing good and leaves the reckoning to Allah.

<!-- story:end -->

## For Teens, Ages 13+

<!-- reader:start audience="13+" estimated_minutes="5.0" -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

A photo lands in the class group chat: a classmate praying in an aisle of the library. Someone adds a snarky caption, and the laughing emojis pour in. Nobody says anything all that terrible, but the message is clear: hold on to your faith and you become the joke. And there you are, staring at the screen. Do you send a laughing emoji so you won't be "the weird one," do you stay quiet, or do you do something else?

This lesson reads ayat from Surah al-Mutaffifin that paint this very scene with startling precision, and then carry us to another gathering entirely: believers "on canopied couches, gazing," in a Jannah whose couches are raised high, whose cushions are lined in rows, and whose people sit facing one another.[^1][^4][^6]

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.terms" kind="terms" -->

<!-- terminology:start source_id="quran-83-22-36" -->

- **`Relishing it (fakihin)`** — delighted and pleased with themselves over what they had done, as as-Sa'di says; for them, mockery was entertainment.[^3]

- **`Repaid (thuwwiba)`** — recompensed; the question "Have the disbelievers not been repaid?" means that they have indeed been recompensed for what they used to do, as Ibn Kathir says.[^1]

<!-- terminology:end -->

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.primary-story" kind="primary_story" -->

### A Story from the Qur'an: Who Owns the Final Seat?

<!-- story:start audience="13+" role="primary" type="quranic" source_id="quran-83-22-36" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true story from the Noble Qur'an, and the explanations in it come from the commentators of the Qur'an.**

The ayat open with the ending before the beginning: "The truly righteous will be in bliss, on canopied couches, gazing." The *ara'ik*, as at-Tabari reports from Ibn Abbas, are couches within curtained canopies. Ibn Kathir gives two views on "gazing": they gaze at their kingdom, which never runs out, or they gaze at their Lord.[^1][^2]

<!-- retelling:start source_id="quran-83-22-36" audience="13+" -->

Then the ayat return to this world and describe the mockery in four moves: laughing at the believers; winking at one another as they pass; heading home on a high; and passing judgment on them: "These people have surely gone astray!" Ibn Kathir points out that the mockers were themselves living in blessings, yet they did not give thanks for them; "rather, they busied themselves with the believers, belittling them and envying them."[^1]

Then comes a short sentence that exposes the root of the problem: "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them." No one had put them in charge of monitoring the believers, but they appointed themselves judges anyway. Here Ibn Kathir cites ayat from Surah al-Mu'minun stating that Allah rewarded those who were mocked "for their patience."[^1]

Then the scene flips: "So today it is those who believed who laugh at the disbelievers, on canopied couches, gazing. Have the disbelievers not been repaid for what they used to do?" As-Sa'di says they were recompensed in kind for what they did, "as justice and wisdom from Allah."[^3]

<!-- retelling:end -->

Mockery gives the mockers a temporary seat at the head of the room, and standing firm may cost you your place in the group. But the ayat say the final seat is not in the hands of the people posting the comments. And turning the two gatherings upside down is Allah's doing on the Day of Resurrection; it is not a license for you, today, to mock the one who mocked you.

<!-- story:end -->

### Discussion Questions

1. Why can a laughing emoji in a group chat count as taking part in mockery, even if you never typed a word?
2. What does "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them" reveal about the mindset of a mocker?
3. How do you tell the difference between waiting for Allah's justice on the Day of Resurrection and gloating or wanting revenge?
4. What does "facing one another" mean in the description of the gatherings of Jannah, and what would a group of friends who "face one another" look like today?
5. What social price are you willing to pay so that you are not one of those who wink at others?

<!-- unit:end -->

### Activity: My Three-Step Plan for Group Chats and Groups

<!-- activity:start audience="13+" concept_id="lesson.044.activity.dont-laugh-redirect-sit-beside" -->

Pick a real group where mockery keeps happening: your class group chat, your team or your lunch table. In a private notebook, write out your plan:

- I won't send a laughing emoji, and I won't repost anything that makes fun of someone.
- A ready-made redirecting message that attacks no one, such as: "Okay, who's ready for the game?"
- A private message to the person who was mocked, or a seat right next to them.

Put it into practice for seven days and log every incident without names: what happened, which step you managed and what it cost you. Then write a paragraph: did you lose a seat in the group, and what did you gain?

### The Du'a of the Prophet, Peace and Blessings Be upon Him, When Going to Bed

<!-- bedtime-dua:start audience="13+" id="lesson.044.dua.in-your-name-my-lord-i-lay-my-side" provenance="sunnah" source_id="bukhari-6320" attribution="prophetic" -->

It is authentically reported that the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, instructed whoever goes to bed to shake out the bed and then say:

> **بِاسْمِكَ رَبِّ وَضَعْتُ جَنْبِي، وَبِكَ أَرْفَعُهُ، إِنْ أَمْسَكْتَ نَفْسِي فَارْحَمْهَا، وَإِنْ أَرْسَلْتَهَا فَاحْفَظْهَا بِمَا تَحْفَظُ بِهِ عِبَادَكَ الصَّالِحِينَ.**[^7]
>
> *"In Your name, my Lord, I lay down my side, and by You I raise it up. If You keep my soul, have mercy on it; and if You send it back, protect it as You protect Your righteous servants."*
>
> What it means: In Your name, Lord, I lay my side down, and by You I raise it. If You take my soul in my sleep, have mercy on it; and if You give it back, protect it as You protect Your righteous servants. So you fall asleep having handed your day over to your Lord, along with everyone's laughter and everyone's approval.[^7]

## Detailed Teaching Plans

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="adults" minutes="60" -->

### Adults — 60 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The learner narrates the scene of al-Mutaffifin 83:22-36 with its two gatherings; explains the meanings of the canopied couches, the raised couches, the couches woven with gold, the cushions and the carpets from the statements of Ibn Abbas and Mujahid as transmitted by Ibn Kathir and at-Tabari; sets out the two views on "gazing"; draws out what is meant by "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them," "well pleased with their striving" and "facing one another"; distinguishes Allah's just recompense from repaying mockery in kind; and designs a three-step plan for a real gathering.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** A copy of the lesson for each learner; a mushaf; a board divided into two columns headed "The gathering in this world" and "The gathering in the Hereafter"; a weekly log sheet with the columns "Incident," "Step used" and "Effect"; an exit card.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher reads the tafsir of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di on al-Mutaffifin 83:22-36, al-Ghashiyah 88:8-16 and al-Waqi'ah 56:15-16, and at-Tabari's tafsir on al-Mutaffifin 83:23 and al-Waqi'ah 56:15. The teacher also prepares a neutral example of a gathering of mockery from work or family life, without names.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** The teacher asks: "Think of a gathering where everyone laughed at one person. Where was that person sitting? And where were you sitting?" The teacher listens without commenting.

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 20 minutes:** Read al-Mutaffifin 83:22-36 in full while learners fill in the two columns (8 minutes). Then the tafsir of the canopied couches and the two views on "gazing," with as-Sa'di's reconciliation of them (4 minutes). Then al-Ghashiyah 88:8-16 and the meanings of its words (4 minutes). Then al-Waqi'ah 56:15-16 and the meaning of facing one another (4 minutes).

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 10 minutes:** The teacher leads a discussion: why did Ibn Kathir treat "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them" as the key to understanding the mockers? And why is the reversal of the two gatherings attributed to Allah on the Day of Resurrection, and not to the believer today? The teacher makes clear that linking the canopied couches to the restoration of dignity is an educational reflection of this lesson, based on the order of the ayat, and not a transmitted scholarly statement.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 15 minutes:** Each learner chooses a real gathering, writes out the three steps and composes the redirecting line, then practices it with a partner in a short role-play, and the two trade one suggestion for improving it.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 10 minutes:** Exit card: "What do *ara'ik*, *namariq* and *zarabi* mean?", "Give the two views on 'gazing'," and "What redirecting line will you use?" Then read the du'a for going to bed from al-Bukhari 6320.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For beginners, cover only ayat 29-35 of al-Mutaffifin and ayat 15-16 of al-Waqi'ah. Ask advanced learners for a table comparing the words used for the seats of Jannah in al-Mutaffifin 83:23, al-Ghashiyah 88:13-16, al-Waqi'ah 56:15-16, al-Kahf 18:31 and Ya-Sin 36:56, with one documented statement from a commentator for each word.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not use the lesson to label particular people as criminals or disbelievers; the ayat describe those who mock faith, and passing judgment on individuals is not the subject of this lesson. Point out that the believers' laughter on the Day of Resurrection is a recompense from Allah and not a model for the believer's conduct in this world. Do not assert anything about the couches of Jannah beyond what is in the text and the statements of the commentators, for they belong to the unseen. The hadith "Their height is as the distance between heaven and earth," cited in explaining "and raised furnishings," is weak and should not be relied on.[^8] Anyone suffering repeated or harmful bullying should be directed to seek help from the responsible authority; the plan alone is not enough for them.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="4-7" minutes="25" -->

### Children Ages 4-7 — 25 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The child says that in Jannah there are high couches, cushions lined up in rows and lots of carpets; retells that the believers were laughed at in this world and that Allah honored them with the special couches; acts out the three steps in role-play; and says or repeats the du'a for going to bed.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Two or three cushions; a piece of soft fabric to spread out like a carpet; two small toys; a sheet of paper for each child; crayons; the du'a card.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** Before the children come in, the caregiver sets up a corner with the cushions in a row and the carpet spread out, practices telling the story calmly, and gets ready a gentle voice for the "sad" toy.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 3 minutes:** The children come in and find the cushions already lined up. The caregiver asks: "Who lined up these cushions for you? Did you have to work hard to arrange them?" Then says: "In Jannah there are cushions lined up in rows, all ready for the people who live there."

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Reading the Evidence — 7 minutes:** The caregiver reads the introduction and then the story, asks the children to repeat "*'ala al-ara'iki yanzurun*: on the special couches, gazing," and points upward whenever the high couches are mentioned.

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 4 minutes:** The caregiver asks the unit's questions and explains that it was Allah who honored the believers, and that we don't laugh at anyone, even if someone laughs at us.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 7 minutes:** The children take turns doing the Friendly Cushion activity. Then each child takes home a sheet of paper on which to draw a cushion every time they sit beside a lonely child over the next three days.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 4 minutes:** The caregiver notices whether the child can name the three steps in order. Then the children pretend to shake out their beds with the edge of their clothes, and repeat the du'a.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For the youngest, the single step "I won't laugh, and I'll sit beside them" is enough. Ask older children to memorize the first sentence of the du'a.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not mention punishment or any details of what happens to the mockers. Do not ask a child to name, in front of others, a child who makes fun of them. If a child says they are laughed at all the time, gently let the parents know. And do not present the bed or couch in Jannah as exactly like the bed at home.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="8-12" minutes="40" -->

### Children Ages 8-12 — 40 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The student narrates the two gatherings in al-Mutaffifin 83:22-36; defines the four terms; explains the meaning of "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them"; explains why a believer does not answer mockery in kind; and makes the three-step card and uses it for five days.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Term cards; a piece of card stock for each student; a mushaf; two drawn pictures: a gathering where one person has backs turned on him, and a gathering of faces turned toward one another.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher prepares three short scenarios of mockery (about clothing, about prayer, about an accent), along with suggested redirecting lines for students who need them.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** The teacher reads the story of Yusuf from the introduction, pauses at his question, and asks students to write, without names, what they would have done.

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 12 minutes:** Read the terms (3 minutes), then the full story while students fill in the columns "The first gathering" and "The second gathering," pointing out the difference between the text of the ayah and the words of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di (9 minutes).

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 8 minutes:** Show the two pictures and read Ibn Kathir's comment on "facing one another": "No one is behind anyone." Then hand out the three scenarios to groups, and each group acts out the three steps.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 10 minutes:** Each student makes a card as described in the activity unit, writes a redirecting line and practices it with a partner.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 5 minutes:** Each student completes: "In this world the mockers used to... and on the Day of Resurrection the believers will..." and "The redirecting line I will use is...". Then read the du'a for going to bed. After five days, anyone who wishes can share their two sentences.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For students who struggle, the first and third steps are enough. Ask advanced students to memorize al-Mutaffifin 83:29-35 and explain why "on canopied couches, gazing" is repeated.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not show students' cards to their classmates. Do not name any student as a mocker. Distinguish passing teasing from repeated bullying, which needs the school and the parents to step in. Do not ask a student to confront a group that is physically hurting them; the third step can be a kind message later on.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="13+" minutes="50" -->

### Teens, Ages 13+ — 50 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The student analyzes the four moves of mockery in al-Mutaffifin 83:29-32; explains what "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them" reveals about the mindset of a mocker; distinguishes waiting for Allah's justice from gloating; connects "facing one another" to the culture of their own group; and completes their three-step plan over seven days.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** A copy of the lesson; a private notebook that is not collected; a mushaf; cards for anonymous answers; a made-up screenshot of a group chat with no real names or photos.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher reviews the tafsir of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di on al-Mutaffifin 83:29-36 and prepares examples of redirecting messages that attack no one and do not preach in front of everyone.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** The teacher asks: "Is a laughing emoji taking part?" Students write anonymous answers on the cards.

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 12 minutes:** Read the introduction and the two terms, then the story, with three pauses: the ending placed at the start of the passage; the four moves of mockery and Ibn Kathir's comment on envy; and then the reversal of the gathering and as-Sa'di's comment on justice.

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 13 minutes:** Group discussion of questions 1, 2 and 3, followed by brief presentations. The teacher steers students toward the point that standing firm does not mean preaching in public or attacking anyone, and that sometimes the strongest step is a private message.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 12 minutes:** Each student writes their three-step plan in their notebook, composes the redirecting message and tests it on a partner: does it sound natural, or forced?

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 8 minutes:** Each student writes three sentences: which move they most often fall into, what "Yet they had not been sent as guardians over them" means for them, and what their ready-made message is. A week later, the teacher follows up with a general question, without asking for the notebooks. Then read the du'a for going to bed.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** Students who find written logging hard may keep private voice notes. Ask advanced students for a written reflection on as-Sa'di's comment on "facing one another," "from the purity of their hearts, the beauty of their manners, and their hearts being turned toward one another," and on what keeps that from happening in their own groups.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not ask anyone to reveal real messages or names. Do not encourage a student into a public confrontation that could expose them to harm. Point out that repeated cyberbullying should be reported to parents or the school. And waiting for Allah's justice must not be understood as wishing punishment on particular classmates; a believer hopes that they will be guided.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->
