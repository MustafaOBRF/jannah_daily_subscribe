# The Trees, Gardens and Shade of Jannah

## Lesson Objectives and Outcomes

After this lesson, the learner will be able to:

- Retell, in order, the parable of the owner of the two gardens in Surah al-Kahf (18:32-44): two gardens of grapevines bordered by date palms, with crops between them and a river running through; then their owner's boasting and his words "I do not think this will ever perish"; then the advice of his believing companion; then the ruin of the fruit and its owner's regret.
- Compare the garden of this world, which perishes, with the gardens of Jannah, of which Allah said, "Its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade" (ar-Ra'd 13:35), together with Ibn Kathir's comment that its shade "neither passes away nor shrinks."
- Cite as evidence the hadith of al-Bukhari (3251) and its parallels about the tree in whose shade a rider travels for a hundred years without crossing it, and the hadith of at-Tirmidhi (2525): "There is no tree in Jannah but that its trunk is of gold."
- Explain the etiquette the story teaches, as set out by Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di: whoever is delighted by something in their wealth or circumstances should say, "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah," and so credit the blessing to the One who bestowed it.
- Carry out the "Shifting Shade, Shade Extended" activity: observing the shade of a real tree at three times in a single day and recording how it moves and shrinks, then saying "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah" whenever a blessing delights them, for a set number of days, and keeping count.
- Close the day with a lesson-authored du'a that praises Allah for His gifts and asks Him for the Jannah whose fruit and shade are everlasting.

## Academic Section for Adults

<!-- reader:start audience="adults" estimated_minutes="6.5" -->

<!-- unit:start id="adults.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

Every garden in this world, however beautiful, lives under three threats: drought that strikes its water, blight that strikes its fruit, and time, which shifts its shade and then takes it away altogether. And yet we stand before what we own and sometimes imagine that it will last, and that we made it by our own strength.

This lesson reads a Qur'anic account of two gardens that reached the height of worldly splendor and were then suddenly destroyed. Beside it, the lesson sets revelation's description of the gardens of Jannah: fruit that never ends, shade that never passes, and a tree in whose shade a rider travels for a hundred years without crossing it. One question joins the two pictures: how should I look at what I have been given today?

## Central Evidence

<!-- evidence:start id="quran-18-32-44" kind="quran" mode="canonical" -->

### The Parable of the Two Men and the Two Gardens

> **{وَاضْرِبْ لَهُمْ مَثَلًا رَجُلَيْنِ جَعَلْنَا لِأَحَدِهِمَا جَنَّتَيْنِ مِنْ أَعْنَابٍ وَحَفَفْنَاهُمَا بِنَخْلٍ وَجَعَلْنَا بَيْنَهُمَا زَرْعًا ۝ كِلْتَا الْجَنَّتَيْنِ آتَتْ أُكُلَهَا وَلَمْ تَظْلِمْ مِنْهُ شَيْئًا ۚ وَفَجَّرْنَا خِلَالَهُمَا نَهَرًا ۝ وَكَانَ لَهُ ثَمَرٌ فَقَالَ لِصَاحِبِهِ وَهُوَ يُحَاوِرُهُ أَنَا أَكْثَرُ مِنْكَ مَالًا وَأَعَزُّ نَفَرًا ۝ وَدَخَلَ جَنَّتَهُ وَهُوَ ظَالِمٌ لِنَفْسِهِ قَالَ مَا أَظُنُّ أَنْ تَبِيدَ هَٰذِهِ أَبَدًا ۝ وَمَا أَظُنُّ السَّاعَةَ قَائِمَةً وَلَئِنْ رُدِدْتُ إِلَىٰ رَبِّي لَأَجِدَنَّ خَيْرًا مِنْهَا مُنْقَلَبًا ۝ قَالَ لَهُ صَاحِبُهُ وَهُوَ يُحَاوِرُهُ أَكَفَرْتَ بِالَّذِي خَلَقَكَ مِنْ تُرَابٍ ثُمَّ مِنْ نُطْفَةٍ ثُمَّ سَوَّاكَ رَجُلًا ۝ لَٰكِنَّا هُوَ اللَّهُ رَبِّي وَلَا أُشْرِكُ بِرَبِّي أَحَدًا ۝ وَلَوْلَا إِذْ دَخَلْتَ جَنَّتَكَ قُلْتَ مَا شَاءَ اللَّهُ لَا قُوَّةَ إِلَّا بِاللَّهِ ۚ إِنْ تَرَنِ أَنَا أَقَلَّ مِنْكَ مَالًا وَوَلَدًا ۝ فَعَسَىٰ رَبِّي أَنْ يُؤْتِيَنِ خَيْرًا مِنْ جَنَّتِكَ وَيُرْسِلَ عَلَيْهَا حُسْبَانًا مِنَ السَّمَاءِ فَتُصْبِحَ صَعِيدًا زَلَقًا ۝ أَوْ يُصْبِحَ مَاؤُهَا غَوْرًا فَلَنْ تَسْتَطِيعَ لَهُ طَلَبًا ۝ وَأُحِيطَ بِثَمَرِهِ فَأَصْبَحَ يُقَلِّبُ كَفَّيْهِ عَلَىٰ مَا أَنْفَقَ فِيهَا وَهِيَ خَاوِيَةٌ عَلَىٰ عُرُوشِهَا وَيَقُولُ يَا لَيْتَنِي لَمْ أُشْرِكْ بِرَبِّي أَحَدًا ۝ وَلَمْ تَكُنْ لَهُ فِئَةٌ يَنْصُرُونَهُ مِنْ دُونِ اللَّهِ وَمَا كَانَ مُنْتَصِرًا ۝ هُنَالِكَ الْوَلَايَةُ لِلَّهِ الْحَقِّ ۚ هُوَ خَيْرٌ ثَوَابًا وَخَيْرٌ عُقْبًا}** [الكهف: ٣٢-٤٤][^1]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> **"Set forth for them the parable of two men. To one of them We gave two gardens of grapevines, bordered them with date palms, and placed crops between them. Both gardens yielded their fruit and fell short in nothing, and We made a river gush forth between them. And he had abundant fruit, so he said to his companion, while talking with him, 'I have more wealth than you, and a mightier band of men.' And he entered his garden, wronging himself. He said, 'I do not think this will ever perish. Nor do I think the Hour will ever come. And even if I am brought back to my Lord, I will surely find something better than this to return to.' His companion said to him, while talking with him, 'Do you disbelieve in the One who created you from dust, then from a drop of fluid, then shaped you into a man? But as for me, He is Allah, my Lord, and I will not associate anyone with my Lord. If only, when you entered your garden, you had said, "This is what Allah has willed; there is no power except through Allah." Though you see me as having less than you in wealth and children, it may be that my Lord will give me something better than your garden, and send down upon it a reckoning from the sky, so that it becomes barren, slippery ground, or its water sinks deep into the earth, so that you will never be able to reach it.' And ruin closed in upon his fruit, and he was left wringing his hands over all he had spent on it, as it lay collapsed upon its trellises, and he said, 'If only I had not associated anyone with my Lord!' He had no band of men to help him besides Allah, nor could he help himself. There, all protection belongs to Allah, the True. He is best in reward and best in outcome."** (al-Kahf 18:32-44)[^1]

#### Scholarly Explanation

As-Sa'di says that Allah gave the man "two fine gardens," with the grapevines in their middle and date palms bordering them, and crops between the trees; and that "fell short in nothing" means "they did not hold back the least part of their yield." Then he says: "This is the very utmost of worldly splendor in cultivation, and that is why this man was deluded by them, swaggered and boasted, and forgot his Hereafter."[^2] On "I do not think this will ever perish," Ibn Kathir says that when the man saw the crops, the fruit, the trees and the rivers, "he thought they would never pass away, never run out, never be destroyed and never be ruined," and that this came from "his lack of sense, the weakness of his certainty in Allah, his infatuation with the life of this world and its adornment, and his disbelief in the Hereafter."[^1]

On "If only, when you entered your garden," Ibn Kathir says: "Why, when it delighted you as you entered it and looked at it, did you not praise Allah for the blessing He had given you?" He also reports from some of the early Muslims (the Salaf) that whoever is delighted by something in his circumstances, his wealth or his children should say, "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah." And he reports from Qatadah on "wringing his hands": "He clapped his palms together in sorrow and anguish over the wealth that had been taken from him."[^1] In drawing the lessons of the story together, as-Sa'di says that when a servant of Allah is delighted by something in his wealth or children, he ought "to credit the blessing to the One who granted it and bestowed it," and to say, "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah," "so that he is grateful to Allah and takes the means for His blessing to remain with him."[^2]

#### Lesson Explanation

The man's sin was not that he owned a beautiful garden. It was that he credited permanence to something that does not last, and strength to himself. The story says it in the language of trees: every garden in this world can perish, and every blessing in it is kept safe by thanking the One who gave it.

### "Its Fruit Is Everlasting, and So Is Its Shade"

> **{مَثَلُ الْجَنَّةِ الَّتِي وُعِدَ الْمُتَّقُونَ ۖ تَجْرِي مِنْ تَحْتِهَا الْأَنْهَارُ ۖ أُكُلُهَا دَائِمٌ وَظِلُّهَا ۚ تِلْكَ عُقْبَى الَّذِينَ اتَّقَوْا ۖ وَعُقْبَى الْكَافِرِينَ النَّارُ}** [الرعد: ٣٥][^3]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> **"The likeness of the Garden promised to those who are mindful of Allah: rivers flow beneath it; its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade. That is the final end of those who were mindful of Allah, and the final end of the disbelievers is the Fire."** (ar-Ra'd 13:35)[^3]

#### Scholarly Explanation

Ibn Kathir says that "its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade" means it holds food, fruit and drink "that never cease and never come to an end," and then adds: "Likewise its shade neither passes away nor shrinks."[^3] As-Sa'di says that the rivers water "those gardens and trees, so they bear every kind of fruit," and that "its fruit and its shade" are "everlasting as well."[^2]

#### Lesson Explanation

Set the two ayat side by side. The owner of the two gardens said, "I do not think this will ever perish," and it perished. The Garden promised to the mindful is the one that Allah Himself describes as everlasting: its fruit never runs out, and its shade never slips away from you or shrinks.

<!-- evidence:end -->

<!-- evidence:start id="bukhari-3251" kind="hadith" mode="canonical" -->

### A Tree in Whose Shade a Rider Travels

> عَنْ أَنَسِ بْنِ مَالِكٍ رضي الله عنه، عَنِ النَّبِيِّ ﷺ قَالَ: **«إِنَّ فِي الْجَنَّةِ لَشَجَرَةً يَسِيرُ الرَّاكِبُ فِي ظِلِّهَا مِائَةَ عَامٍ لَا يَقْطَعُهَا»**.[^4]
>
> وَفِي رِوَايَةِ أَبِي سَعِيدٍ الْخُدْرِيِّ رضي الله عنه عَنِ النَّبِيِّ ﷺ: **«إِنَّ فِي الْجَنَّةِ شَجَرَةً يَسِيرُ الرَّاكِبُ الْجَوَادَ الْمُضَمَّرَ السَّرِيعَ مِائَةَ عَامٍ مَا يَقْطَعُهَا»**.[^4]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> On the authority of Anas ibn Malik, may Allah be pleased with him, the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said: **"In Jannah there is a tree in whose shade a rider travels for a hundred years without crossing it."**[^4]
>
> And in the narration of Abu Sa'id al-Khudri, may Allah be pleased with him, from the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him: **"In Jannah there is a tree; a rider on a swift, lean-trained racehorse could travel for a hundred years and still not cross it."**[^4]

#### Scholarly Explanation

An-Nawawi says: "The scholars said: what is meant by its shade is its shelter and covering, that is, what its branches cover." And *al-jawad al-mudammar* is a horse "that has been made lean so that it runs all the harder," that is, a horse trained for speed.[^4]

<!-- evidence:end -->

<!-- evidence:start id="tirmidhi-2525" kind="hadith" mode="canonical" -->

### "And Its Trunk Is of Gold"

> عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ رضي الله عنه قَالَ: قَالَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ: **«مَا فِي الْجَنَّةِ شَجَرَةٌ إِلَّا وَسَاقُهَا مِنْ ذَهَبٍ»**.[^5]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> On the authority of Abu Hurayrah, may Allah be pleased with him, who said: The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: **"There is no tree in Jannah but that its trunk is of gold."**[^5]

#### Lesson Explanation

The *saq* of a tree is its trunk. The trees of this world stand on trunks of wood that time eats away; every tree of Jannah stands on a trunk of gold. The tree of Tuba, the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary (Sidrat al-Muntaha), and the details of the fruits of Jannah each have a lesson of their own.

<!-- evidence:end -->

### How Do These Texts Fit Together?

The story in al-Kahf shows the finest a worldly garden can become, and then how it ends. The ayah of ar-Ra'd describes a Garden whose fruit and shade have no end. And the two hadiths bring within reach the vastness of a single tree's shade and the nobility of the trunks that hold the trees up. Between the two pictures lies a practical courtesy: whoever looks at their garden, their home or their success should say, "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah," and then make what they have into a path toward the shade that never passes.

## Questions for Understanding and Reflection

### Activity: Shifting Shade, Shade Extended

<!-- activity:start audience="adults" concept_id="lesson.053.activity.trace-the-moving-shade-say-mashallah" -->

Choose a real tree near you and photograph its shade from the same spot three times in one day: in the morning, near midday, and shortly before sunset. Do not look directly at the sun. Under each photo, note which way the shade was pointing and how much it had widened or narrowed; beside it, write down a worldly blessing of yours that changes in the same way. Then, for seven days, say "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah" whenever something in your wealth, your home, your work or your children delights you, and tally the count each evening. At the end, write a paragraph: what has changed in the way you look at what you own?

## For Children Ages 4 to 7

<!-- unit:start id="4-7.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

Have you ever sat under a tree on a hot day? Its shade is so cool and nice! But if you sit there for a long time, the sun moves, so the shade moves too, and you have to get up and scoot over.

The shade of the trees in Jannah never goes away. Allah said about Jannah, "Its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade." That means its food never runs out, and its shade never runs out.[^3] Our Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, told us that in Jannah there is a very, very big tree. If someone rode a fast horse through its shade for a hundred years, they still would not reach the end of it![^4] And he, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "There is no tree in Jannah but that its trunk is of gold."[^5]

### From the Qur'an: The Man with Two Gardens

<!-- story:start audience="4-7" role="primary" type="quranic" source_id="quran-18-32-44" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true story from the Noble Qur'an. It is not a made-up story.**

In Surah al-Kahf, Allah tells us about two men.

<!-- retelling:start source_id="quran-18-32-44" audience="4-7" -->

Allah gave the first man two beautiful gardens. They had lots and lots of grapes, tall date palms all around them, green crops in between, and a river running right through the middle. The trees were heavy with fruit.

The man was happy, but he became proud. He said to his friend, "I have more money than you!" Then he walked into his garden and said, "I don't think this garden will ever go away!" He forgot that Allah was the One who gave it to him.

His friend, who believed in Allah, said to him kindly: Why didn't you say, when you walked into your garden, "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah"? That means: "All of this is from Allah, and we have no strength except from Allah."

But the man did not listen. One morning he woke up and found his fruit ruined and his garden fallen down onto its wooden posts. He kept turning his hands over and over, sad about everything he had spent on it, and he said, "If only I had not associated anyone with my Lord!"[^1]

<!-- retelling:end -->

A garden in this world can go away, but the garden of Jannah never goes away. When you love something you have, say what the believing friend said: "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah."

### Activity: Where Did the Shadow Go?

<!-- activity:start audience="4-7" concept_id="lesson.053.activity.trace-the-moving-shade-say-mashallah" -->

Go outside with your mom or dad to a tree or a pole in the yard or in front of your home. In the morning, put a small pebble at the tip of its shadow. Near lunchtime, put down a second pebble, and before sunset, a third one. Don't look at the sun! Now look: did the shadow stay in the same place? Draw the tree and the three pebbles on a piece of paper. For the next three days, every time you love a toy, a drawing or some yummy food, say "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah," and draw a little leaf on your paper.

## For Children Ages 8 to 12

<!-- reader:start audience="8-12" estimated_minutes="5.0" -->

<!-- unit:start id="8-12.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

Your school team has just finished its garden project: beds of mint, little lemon trees, and a bench under a big tree. In the group photo, one classmate stood right up front and said, "If it weren't for my idea, none of this would have worked!" A week later, after a heat wave, half the seedlings had wilted, because nobody watered them over the break.

In this lesson we read a story from Surah al-Kahf about a man who thought his garden would never disappear. Then we get to know the trees of Jannah, about which Allah said, "Its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade."[^3] The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said: "In Jannah there is a tree in whose shade a rider travels for a hundred years without crossing it."[^4] And he said: "There is no tree in Jannah but that its trunk is of gold."[^5]

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="8-12.terms" kind="terms" -->

<!-- terminology:start source_id="quran-18-32-44" -->

- **`Bordered them with date palms (hafafnahuma bi-nakhl)`** — We surrounded the two gardens with date palms on every side, with the grapevines in the middle, as as-Sa'di explains.[^2]
- **`Perish (tabid)`** — to pass away and come to an end; as-Sa'di explains it as "to be cut off and fade away."[^2]
- **`Collapsed upon its trellises (khawiyatun 'ala 'urushiha)`** — fallen down onto the posts that used to hold up the grapevines, and empty of fruit.[^1]
- **`Its fruit (ukuluha)`** — its fruit and its food, which in Jannah never runs out.[^3]

### From the Qur'an: Two Gardens and a Missing Phrase

<!-- story:start audience="8-12" role="primary" type="quranic" source_id="quran-18-32-44" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true story from the Noble Qur'an. Any explanation in it comes from the scholars of tafsir.**

In Surah al-Kahf, Allah sets forth a parable about two men, and He does not tell us their names. As as-Sa'di explains, the lesson lies in the story itself, not in knowing who they were.[^2]

<!-- retelling:start source_id="quran-18-32-44" audience="8-12" -->

Allah gave one of them two gardens of grapevines, with date palms all around them, crops in between, and a river running through the middle. Each garden gave its full harvest, without falling short in anything.

Talking with his companion, the man said, "I have more wealth than you, and a mightier band of men." Then he walked into his garden and said, "I do not think this will ever perish," and he even doubted that the Day of Judgment would come. Ibn Kathir says he thought his garden would never pass away or be ruined because of "his lack of sense, and the weakness of his certainty in Allah."[^1]

His believing companion answered him: Do you disbelieve in the One who created you from dust? As for me, Allah is my Lord. Then he said to him: "If only, when you entered your garden, you had said, 'Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah: this is what Allah has willed; there is no power except through Allah.'" And he warned him that Allah might send something upon the garden that would turn it into bare, slippery ground where nothing grows, or make its water sink away deep into the earth.

Then ruin closed in on his fruit. He was left wringing his hands in regret over all he had spent, while his garden lay collapsed on its trellises, and he said, "If only I had not associated anyone with my Lord!" And he found no one to help him.[^1]

<!-- retelling:end -->

Notice the difference. A garden in this world can be gone between one morning and the next evening, but the gardens of Jannah are described this way: "Its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade." Ibn Kathir says its shade "neither passes away nor shrinks."[^3] The phrase the owner of the two gardens left unsaid is the key to looking at every blessing the right way.

<!-- story:end -->

2. What phrase did his companion advise him to say? What does it mean?
3. How is the shade of the trees in Jannah different from the shade of trees in this world?
4. What does the hadith about the tree in whose shade a rider travels for a hundred years tell us?
5. What is something you sometimes brag about while forgetting that Allah is the One who gave it to you?

### Activity: The Shadow Log

<!-- activity:start audience="8-12" concept_id="lesson.053.activity.trace-the-moving-shade-say-mashallah" -->

Pick a tree at school or near your home. Draw its shadow, or mark the tip of it with chalk, three times in one day: in the morning, near midday, and in the mid-afternoon, after 'Asr. Don't look directly at the sun. Make a table with three columns: the time, which way the shadow points, and whether it got bigger or smaller. Under the table, write one sentence comparing this shade with Allah's words, "Its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade." Then, for five days, say "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah" whenever something you have makes you happy, like a grade, a goal you scored or a drawing, and put a check mark in your table each time. At the end, write two sentences about what you learned.

## For Teens, Ages 13+

<!-- reader:start audience="13+" estimated_minutes="5.5" -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

A photo of a spotless room, a new phone, a test score or the family car, with the caption: "Worked hard for this. All me." There's nothing wrong with being happy about a blessing. But there is a subtle moment when that happiness turns into something else: when you start measuring yourself by what you own, forget who gave it to you, and assume that what you have is here to stay.

Surah al-Kahf captures that moment in a man walking into his garden. Then revelation brings the picture of the garden that never perishes: "Its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade."[^3] The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, says: "In Jannah there is a tree; a rider on a swift, lean-trained racehorse could travel for a hundred years and still not cross it." *Al-mudammar* means a horse trained to run at full speed.[^4] And he says: "There is no tree in Jannah but that its trunk is of gold."[^5]

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.terms" kind="terms" -->

<!-- terminology:start source_id="quran-18-32-44" -->

- **`Wronging himself (wa huwa zalimun li-nafsih)`** — he walked into his garden full of arrogance and denying the Hereafter, as Ibn Kathir says.[^1]
- **`A reckoning from the sky (husbanan min as-sama')`** — a punishment sent down from the sky on the garden, destroying it.[^1]
- **`Barren, slippery ground (sa'idan zalaqa)`** — smooth, bare ground where nothing grows and no foot can keep its grip.[^1]
- **`All protection belongs to Allah, the True (al-walayah lillahi al-haqq)`** — at that point it becomes clear that help and judgment belong to Allah alone, and that whatever is done for Allah "has the better reward and the praiseworthy outcome," as Ibn Kathir says.[^1]

<!-- terminology:end -->

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.primary-story" kind="primary_story" -->

### From the Qur'an: The Moment He Walked Into His Garden

<!-- story:start audience="13+" role="primary" type="quranic" source_id="quran-18-32-44" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true story from the Noble Qur'an. Any explanation in it comes from the scholars of tafsir.**

According to Ibn Kathir, this parable comes in Surah al-Kahf right after the mention of people who were too proud to sit with the poor among the Muslims and who looked down on them because of their own wealth.[^1]

<!-- retelling:start source_id="quran-18-32-44" audience="13+" -->

A man owns two gardens of grapevines, ringed with date palms, with crops between them and a river cutting through. As-Sa'di calls this "the very utmost of worldly splendor in cultivation," and says that this is why "this man was deluded by them, swaggered and boasted, and forgot his Hereafter."[^2]

In conversation with his companion, he says: "I have more wealth than you, and a mightier band of men." Then he walks into his garden and says: "I do not think this will ever perish. Nor do I think the Hour will ever come." He even claims that if he were brought back to his Lord, he would find something better there.

His companion answers calmly and firmly. He reminds him that he was created from dust, declares his own belief in the One God, and then puts his finger on that exact moment: "If only, when you entered your garden, you had said, 'Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah: this is what Allah has willed; there is no power except through Allah.'" Ibn Kathir explains: why, when it delighted you, did you not "praise Allah for the blessing He had given you"?[^1]

Then ruin closes in on the fruit. He is left wringing his hands over everything he spent, while the garden lies collapsed on its trellises, and he says, "If only I had not associated anyone with my Lord!" No one comes to his aid. And the closing line: "There, all protection belongs to Allah, the True. He is best in reward and best in outcome."[^1]

<!-- retelling:end -->

As-Sa'di says that when a servant of Allah is delighted by something in his wealth or children, he ought "to credit the blessing to the One who granted it and bestowed it," so that he will be "grateful to Allah and take the means for His blessing to remain with him."[^2] On the other side stands the garden of Jannah, which Allah describes as everlasting, and whose shade, in Ibn Kathir's words, "neither passes away nor shrinks."[^3] The difference between the two gardens is not only in their beauty. It is in whether they last.

<!-- story:end -->

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.questions" kind="questions" -->

### Discussion Questions

1. Why was the moment of "walking into the garden" the very moment the believing companion focused on?
2. What is the difference between being happy about a blessing and showing it off to others?
3. How does "I do not think this will ever perish" show up in young people's lives today, even when no one says it out loud?
4. What does the picture of a tree whose shade a racehorse cannot cross in a hundred years add to your understanding of the words "and so is its shade"?
5. Which blessing will you say "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah" over this week?

<!-- unit:end -->

### Activity: Shade for a Day, Shade Forever

<!-- activity:start audience="13+" concept_id="lesson.053.activity.trace-the-moving-shade-say-mashallah" -->

Pick a tree you pass every day and photograph its shade from the same spot three times in one day: in the morning, near midday, and shortly before sunset, without looking directly at the sun. Put the three photos side by side, and under each one write a blessing in your life that changes in the same way: your health, your standing with your friends, a skill, your followers. Then, for seven days, say "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah" whenever something about yourself or something you own impresses you, and log the count in a private notebook without telling anyone. At the end, write a paragraph: when did you almost say "I did this all by myself," and what changed when you credited the blessing to Allah?

## Detailed Teaching Plans

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="adults" minutes="60" -->

### Adults — 60 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The learner retells the parable of al-Kahf 18:32-44 in order; states the reasons the owner of the two gardens was deluded, in the words of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di; contrasts "I do not think this will ever perish" with "Its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade"; explains the hadith of the tree (al-Bukhari 3251 and its parallels) through an-Nawawi's comment, and the hadith of at-Tirmidhi 2525; and plans a week of saying "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah" whenever a blessing delights them.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** A copy of the lesson for each learner; a mushaf; a board with two columns headed "The Garden in al-Kahf" and "The Gardens of Jannah"; three photos of the shade of one tree at three times of day, taken in advance by the teacher; the activity sheet; an exit card.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher reads the tafsir of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di on al-Kahf 18:32-44 and ar-Ra'd 13:35, and an-Nawawi's commentary on Muslim 2826-2828, and photographs the shade of a nearby tree at three times on an earlier day.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** The teacher shows the three photos and asks: "What changed between the first photo and the third? And what in your life changes in the same way without your noticing?"

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 20 minutes:** Read al-Kahf 18:32-44 in full, aloud and clearly; then learners divide it into five scenes: the blessing, the boasting, the advice, the ruin, and the closing (8 minutes). They fill in the "Garden in al-Kahf" column from the words of as-Sa'di and Ibn Kathir (4 minutes). Then read ar-Ra'd 13:35 and fill in the second column (3 minutes). Then the two hadiths, with an-Nawawi's comment on the meaning of the shade and of *al-mudammar* (5 minutes).

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 10 minutes:** The teacher discusses the second and fifth questions, stressing that the story does not condemn owning property or working, but crediting permanence to what is passing and strength to oneself; and that saying "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah" is a Qur'anic courtesy when something delights us, as Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di set out.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 15 minutes:** Under the teacher's three photos, learners write three worldly blessings that change the way the shade changes. Then each learner chooses the tree they will photograph themselves, the situations in which they expect something they have to delight them during the week, and how they will keep their evening count.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 10 minutes:** Exit card: "What phrase did the owner of the two gardens leave unsaid?", "What does 'Its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade' mean according to Ibn Kathir?" and "Which blessing will you practice with?" Then read the lesson-authored closing du'a, pointing out that it is not a transmitted (ma'thur) du'a.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For beginners, ayat 34-35, 39 and 42, ar-Ra'd 13:35 and the hadith of Anas are enough. Ask advanced learners to summarize the lessons as-Sa'di draws from the story in his tafsir of ayah 44, attributing each one to him.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not name the two men: the Qur'an does not name them, and as-Sa'di says that trying to identify them is "an affectation." Do not present the lesson as a condemnation of wealth, farming or work. Do not rely on the marfu' hadith that Ibn Kathir cites from the Musnad of Abu Ya'la, saying that whoever says the phrase will see no harm come to his blessing short of death; Ibn Kathir reports from al-Azdi that it "is not authentic."[^6] Do not claim certainty about which tree the hadith refers to, and do not go into the details of the tree of Tuba, the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary or the fruits of Jannah here; each has a lesson of its own.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="4-7" minutes="25" -->

### Children Ages 4-7 — 25 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The child tells the story of the man with two gardens in three sentences; says that the shade of the trees in Jannah never goes away; repeats "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah"; and lays out the three shadow pebbles with their family and draws them.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** A potted plant or a picture of a big tree; a flashlight; a plastic bunch of grapes or a picture of one; three small pebbles in a bag for each child; paper and crayons; the du'a card.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The educator practices telling the story calmly and without frightening the children, and sends parents a short note explaining the three-pebble activity and the warning not to look at the sun.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 3 minutes:** The educator turns off the light and moves the flashlight around the plant, so the children watch its shadow move. Then the educator asks, "Why did the shadow move?" and says, "In Jannah there is shade that never moves and never goes away."

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Reading the Evidence — 7 minutes:** The educator reads the introduction and then the Qur'anic story, holding up the grapes when the garden is mentioned, asking the children to turn their hands over and over when the man's regret is mentioned, and having them repeat "Ma sha' Allah, la quwwata illa billah."

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 4 minutes:** Ask the unit's questions, and stress that Allah loves those who thank Him for His blessings, and that we say the phrase when we love something we have.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 7 minutes:** The children practice placing pebbles at the tip of the plant's shadow while the flashlight moves, then draw the tree and the pebbles, and take their bag of pebbles home to repeat the activity with the real sun.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 4 minutes:** The educator watches whether a child says the phrase on seeing a friend's drawing or toy. Then read the du'a together, one line at a time, explaining that it is a du'a written for this lesson.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For the youngest, drawing the tree and repeating "Ma sha' Allah" is enough. Ask older children to name what was in the two gardens: grapes, date palms, crops and a river.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not dwell on the man's punishment; it is enough to say that his garden was gone. Do not make children afraid of losing their toys. Remind parents that the child must not look at the sun, and that the activity should be done in a safe place away from the road.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="8-12" minutes="40" -->

### Children Ages 8-12 — 40 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The student retells the scenes of the story in order; defines the four terms; compares the garden in al-Kahf with the gardens of Jannah using ar-Ra'd 13:35; draws a tree's shadow at three times of day; and keeps a five-day record of saying the phrase.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Term cards; five shuffled cards showing the scenes of the story; chalk; a table sheet for each student; a mushaf.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher picks a tree in the schoolyard whose shadow can be tracked, and reads the tafsir of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di on al-Kahf 18:32-44.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** The teacher reads the garden-project story from the introduction and asks: "What was missing in the classmate who said, 'If it weren't for my idea'?"

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 12 minutes:** Read the terms (3 minutes); then the Qur'anic story, while students put the five scene cards in order, pointing out the difference between the text of the ayat and the words of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di (6 minutes); then the ayah of ar-Ra'd and the two hadiths (3 minutes).

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 8 minutes:** In groups, each group writes three differences between the garden in al-Kahf and the gardens of Jannah. Then the teacher discusses the fifth question without requiring anyone to answer aloud.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 10 minutes:** Students go out to the tree, mark the tip of its shadow with chalk and write down the time; they complete the other two observations at recess and in the mid-afternoon, and start their five-day table.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 5 minutes:** Each student completes the sentence: "A garden in this world... but the garden of Jannah..." Then read the closing du'a. After five days, anyone who wishes can share their count.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For students who struggle, two shadow observations and the three main scenes of the story are enough. Ask advanced students to memorize ayah 39 and explain it in their own words.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Never mock a student who loves what they have or is happy about an achievement; the point is to credit the blessing to Allah, not to hide one's joy. No one should look directly at the sun. Never single out a student who once bragged in front of classmates.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="13+" minutes="50" -->

### Teens, Ages 13+ — 50 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The student analyzes the moment of "walking into the garden" in al-Kahf 18:35 and 39 and how it plays out in today's culture of showing off; explains "Its fruit is everlasting, and so is its shade" through Ibn Kathir's comment; connects the hadith of the tree to the meaning of shade extended without end; and completes the three-photo activity and the seven-day log.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Copies of the lesson; a private notebook that is not collected; a mushaf; three photos of a tree's shade at three times of day; cards for anonymous answers.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher reviews the tafsir of Ibn Kathir and as-Sa'di on al-Kahf 18:32-44 and ar-Ra'd 13:35, and an-Nawawi's commentary on the hadith of the tree, and prepares the photos.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** The teacher asks, "When does being happy about what you have turn into showing off over others?" and students write anonymous answers on the cards.

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 12 minutes:** Read the introduction and the terms, then the Qur'anic story, with three pauses: as-Sa'di's words on the splendor of the two gardens; then the believing companion's words in ayah 39 and Ibn Kathir's comment; then the closing line, "There, all protection belongs to Allah, the True."

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 13 minutes:** Group discussion of questions 1, 2 and 3, followed by brief presentations. The teacher points out that the believing companion neither envied his friend nor mocked him; he reminded him of the phrase, and of Allah.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 12 minutes:** Under the teacher's three photos, students write blessings that change the way the shade changes. Then each student chooses the tree they will photograph and writes in their private notebook the situations in which they expect to say the phrase.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 8 minutes:** Each student writes three sentences: the phrase the owner of the two gardens left unsaid, the difference between the shade of this world and the shade of Jannah, and the blessing they will practice with. A week later, the teacher follows up with a general question, without asking to see notebooks. Then read the closing du'a.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** Students who find photography difficult may draw the shade instead. Ask advanced students for a written reflection on as-Sa'di's words: "so that he is grateful to Allah and takes the means for His blessing to remain with him."

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** No one is asked to show their belongings or personal photos to the class. Be mindful of students whose families are going through financial hardship, and never let the lesson turn into a comparison of what students own. Do not claim that anyone who fails to say the phrase will lose their blessing; the marfu' hadith to that effect is not authentic.[^6]
