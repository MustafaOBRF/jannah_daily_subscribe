# Sowing and Planting in Jannah

## Lesson Objectives and Outcomes

After this lesson, the learner will be able to:

- Narrate, in order, the hadith of the man among the people of Jannah who asked his Lord's permission to farm (al-Bukhari 2348 and 7519): the request for permission; the question "Do you not have whatever you wish?"; his answer, "Yes indeed, but I love to farm"; the crop sprouting and being harvested in the blink of an eye, in heaps like mountains; Allah's words, "Take it, son of Adam, for nothing ever fills you"; and the Bedouin's remark that made the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, laugh.
- Connect the hadith to the words of Allah {وَفِيهَا مَا تَشْتَهِيهِ الْأَنْفُسُ وَتَلَذُّ الْأَعْيُنُ}, "and therein is whatever souls desire and whatever delights the eyes" (az-Zukhruf 43:71), and explain that the bliss of Jannah includes the very pleasure of working, with no toil and no waiting.
- State what Ibn Hajar drew from the hadith: that whatever worldly thing is desired in Jannah is possible there, that human souls are made to want more and more, and that the hadith points to the virtue of contentment.
- Explain from the hadith of Anas (al-Bukhari 2320) that what a Muslim plants in this world counts as charity for him whenever a bird, a person or an animal eats from it.
- Carry out the "Sow, Wait, and Share the Yield" activity: planting a real seed, logging the days of waiting and care, then giving away the first of what it produces as charity, and writing down what it taught about patience and contentment.
- Learn by heart the du'a of the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him: «اللَّهُمَّ إِنِّي أَسْأَلُكَ الْهُدَى وَالتُّقَى وَالْعَفَافَ وَالْغِنَى» (Muslim 2721), and understand that the self-sufficiency asked for in it is the richness of the soul.

## Academic Section for Adults

<!-- reader:start audience="adults" estimated_minutes="6.5" -->

<!-- unit:start id="adults.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

Any farmer knows how farming goes in this world: plowing, sowing, watering, then weeks or months of waiting, with the fear of blight or drought hanging over it all, and finally an exhausting harvest. Even so, a great many people love to grow things, and there is a joy all its own in watching a seedling split the soil. Is there farming in Jannah, then? And why would anyone sow who already lives in a bliss that leaves him wanting for nothing?

This lesson looks at farming in Jannah as one expression of Allah's words {وَفِيهَا مَا تَشْتَهِيهِ الْأَنْفُسُ}, "and therein is whatever souls desire," through an authentic hadith about a man among the people of Jannah who asked his Lord's permission to farm. The saplings of Jannah that are earned through dhikr have a lesson of their own, so we will not go over them again here. The lesson adds two practical angles: that farming in this world can itself be charity, and that a soul which nothing ever fills needs to learn contentment here, and to turn its eagerness toward what lasts.

## Central Evidence

<!-- evidence:start id="quran-43-71" kind="quran" mode="canonical" -->

### "And Therein Is Whatever Souls Desire"

> **{يُطَافُ عَلَيْهِمْ بِصِحَافٍ مِنْ ذَهَبٍ وَأَكْوَابٍ ۖ وَفِيهَا مَا تَشْتَهِيهِ الْأَنْفُسُ وَتَلَذُّ الْأَعْيُنُ ۖ وَأَنْتُمْ فِيهَا خَالِدُونَ}** [الزخرف: ٧١][^1]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> **"Platters of gold and goblets will be passed around among them, and therein is whatever souls desire and whatever delights the eyes, and you will abide therein forever."** (az-Zukhruf 43:71)[^1]

#### Scholarly Explanation

On `whatever delights the eyes`, Ibn Kathir said: "That is, fine taste and fragrance and a beautiful sight."[^1] Abu Nu'aym al-Asbahani made this ayah the heading of his chapter "On the Saplings and the Farming," and Ibn al-Qayyim opened his chapter "On the Farming of Jannah" with it.[^5]

#### Lesson Explanation

The ayah is general: everything the soul desires in Jannah is there for it, and it lasts without end. The hadith that follows is a single example that shows how wide that "everything" reaches: even the desire to work with one's hands and to farm falls within it.

<!-- evidence:end -->

<!-- evidence:start id="bukhari-2348" kind="hadith" mode="canonical" -->

### "But I Love to Farm"

> عَنْ أَبِي هُرَيْرَةَ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ: أَنَّ النَّبِيَّ ﷺ كَانَ يَوْمًا يُحَدِّثُ، وَعِنْدَهُ رَجُلٌ مِنْ أَهْلِ الْبَادِيَةِ: **«أَنَّ رَجُلًا مِنْ أَهْلِ الْجَنَّةِ اسْتَأْذَنَ رَبَّهُ فِي الزَّرْعِ، فَقَالَ لَهُ: أَلَسْتَ فِيمَا شِئْتَ؟ قَالَ: بَلَى، وَلَكِنِّي أُحِبُّ أَنْ أَزْرَعَ. قَالَ: فَبَذَرَ، فَبَادَرَ الطَّرْفَ نَبَاتُهُ وَاسْتِوَاؤُهُ وَاسْتِحْصَادُهُ، فَكَانَ أَمْثَالَ الْجِبَالِ، فَيَقُولُ اللَّهُ: دُونَكَ يَا ابْنَ آدَمَ، فَإِنَّهُ لَا يُشْبِعُكَ شَيْءٌ»**. فَقَالَ الْأَعْرَابِيُّ: وَاللَّهِ لَا تَجِدُهُ إِلَّا قُرَشِيًّا أَوْ أَنْصَارِيًّا، فَإِنَّهُمْ أَصْحَابُ زَرْعٍ، وَأَمَّا نَحْنُ فَلَسْنَا بِأَصْحَابِ زَرْعٍ. فَضَحِكَ النَّبِيُّ ﷺ.[^2]
>
> وَفِي رِوَايَةٍ: **«أَوَلَسْتَ فِيمَا شِئْتَ؟ ... فَأَسْرَعَ وَبَذَرَ، فَتَبَادَرَ الطَّرْفَ نَبَاتُهُ وَاسْتِوَاؤُهُ وَاسْتِحْصَادُهُ وَتَكْوِيرُهُ أَمْثَالَ الْجِبَالِ»**، وَفِيهَا: فَقَالَ الْأَعْرَابِيُّ: يَا رَسُولَ اللَّهِ، لَا تَجِدُ هَذَا إِلَّا قُرَشِيًّا أَوْ أَنْصَارِيًّا.[^2]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> Abu Hurayrah, may Allah be pleased with him, reported that one day the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, was speaking, and a man from the desert folk was sitting with him: **"A man from the people of Jannah asked his Lord's permission to farm. He said to him: 'Do you not have whatever you wish?' He said: 'Yes indeed, but I love to farm.' He said: So he sowed, and its sprouting, its ripening and its readiness for harvest outran the blink of an eye, and it stood like mountains. Then Allah says: 'Take it, son of Adam, for nothing ever fills you.'"** The Bedouin said, "By Allah, you will find him to be none other than a man of Quraysh or of the Ansar, for they are farming people; as for us, we are not farming people." And the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, laughed.[^2]
>
> In another narration: **"Do you not have whatever you wish? ... So he hurried and sowed, and its sprouting, its ripening, its readiness for harvest and its heaping up raced the blink of an eye, in heaps like mountains."** And in it: The Bedouin said, "O Messenger of Allah, you will find this to be none other than a man of Quraysh or of the Ansar."[^2]

#### Scholarly Explanation

Ibn Hajar explained that *at-tarf* here means "the movement of the eyelid," and that *takwiruhu* means "gathering it together." The point is that between the sowing and "the completion of the whole affair, the uprooting, the reaping, the winnowing, the gathering and the heaping, there was no more than the space of a glance of the eye"; and *dunaka* means "take it." He relayed from al-Muhallab that among the hadith's lessons is "that everything of the things of this world desired in Jannah is possible there," and he said: "It shows that souls are made to seek more and more of this world; it points to the virtue of contentment and the blameworthiness of greed; and it reports something certain to come in the language of the past tense."[^3] Ibn al-Qayyim said: "Perhaps he asked permission for a crop that he would tend and sow with his own hand, though he had no need of it and had been spared its labor."[^5] Al-Mulla Ali al-Qari took the words "nothing ever fills you" to have been said "by way of rebuke, to show the unseemliness of what he asked for," and noted that the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, laughed "at the Bedouin's quick wit."[^4]

#### Lesson Explanation

The hadith invites five pauses. First: the courtesy of asking permission, even in the Abode of Honor. Second: a question that recalls how complete the blessing already is: "Do you not have whatever you wish?" Third: the pleasure of Jannah takes in the pleasure of the work itself; the man has no need of wheat, but he loves to scatter seed with his own hand. Fourth: what takes months of labor and worry in this world is done there in the blink of an eye, and the yield stands like mountains. Fifth: nothing ever fills the son of Adam. In this world that nature needs contentment to keep it in check, and an eagerness redirected toward the Hereafter. And the Prophet's laughter, peace and blessings be upon him, at the Bedouin's words reminds us that everyone pictures bliss in terms of what they already know, while Jannah is wider than all our imaginings.

<!-- evidence:end -->

<!-- evidence:start id="bukhari-2320" kind="hadith" mode="canonical" -->

### "It Counts as Charity for Him"

> عَنْ أَنَسٍ رَضِيَ اللَّهُ عَنْهُ قَالَ: قَالَ رَسُولُ اللَّهِ ﷺ: **«مَا مِنْ مُسْلِمٍ يَغْرِسُ غَرْسًا، أَوْ يَزْرَعُ زَرْعًا، فَيَأْكُلُ مِنْهُ طَيْرٌ أَوْ إِنْسَانٌ أَوْ بَهِيمَةٌ، إِلَّا كَانَ لَهُ بِهِ صَدَقَةٌ»**.[^6]

<!-- evidence:translation -->

> Anas, may Allah be pleased with him, said: The Messenger of Allah, peace and blessings be upon him, said: **"There is no Muslim who plants a tree or sows a crop, and then a bird, a person or an animal eats from it, except that it counts as charity for him."**[^6]

#### Scholarly Explanation

Ibn Hajar said: "The hadith shows the merit of planting and sowing, and encourages cultivating the earth." He also relayed how al-Qurtubi reconciled it with the reports that censure taking up landed estates: the censure applies to amassing them and being distracted by them from one's religion, while the merit applies to taking them up for a sufficient livelihood or to benefit the Muslims (summarized).[^7]

#### Lesson Explanation

Farming in this world is tiring and slow, but it is never wasted: every grain a bird, a person or an animal eats is recorded as charity. So the believer plants here with the Hereafter in view, and takes care that the farming never turns into a hunger for more that distracts him from his Lord.

<!-- evidence:end -->

## Questions for Understanding and Reflection

1. What does the hadith of the farmer add to the general statement {وَفِيهَا مَا تَشْتَهِيهِ الْأَنْفُسُ}, "and therein is whatever souls desire"?

2. Why did the man ask permission to farm when he already had whatever he wished? How does Ibn al-Qayyim account for it?

3. How did Ibn Hajar and al-Mulla Ali al-Qari understand the words "for nothing ever fills you"? What do the two readings have in common?

4. Where in your own life do you see that the soul is "made to seek more and more"? How does contentment keep it in check?

5. How does the hadith of Anas turn the toil of farming in this world into work for the Hereafter?

6. What do you learn from the Prophet's laughter, peace and blessings be upon him, at the Bedouin's remark?

### Activity: Sow, Wait, and Share the Yield

<!-- activity:start audience="adults" concept_id="lesson.057.activity.sow-wait-and-share-the-yield" -->

Grow something real: fast-growing herb seeds in a pot, a seedling in a garden, or a tree through a trustworthy tree-planting project. Keep a fourteen-day log in a three-column table: the day number; the care you gave (watering, light, weeding); and what you noticed in yourself, whether impatience, boredom or joy. Then share the first thing your planting yields, or its seeds, or its shade, or grain for the birds, intending it as charity, as in the hadith of Anas. Close with a paragraph: What did the waiting teach me about the blessing of Jannah, where a crop is finished in the blink of an eye? And what is the one thing I am always asking "more" of, and where is "enough" for it?

### An Authentic Hadith: The Man Who Loved to Plant

<!-- story:start audience="4-7" role="primary" type="hadith" source_id="bukhari-2348" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true report that al-Bukhari recorded in his Sahih. It is not a made-up story.**

<!-- retelling:start source_id="bukhari-2348" audience="4-7" -->

One day the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, was talking, and a man from the desert was sitting with him. He was one of the people who live out in the open desert.[^2]

The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said that a man in Jannah asked his Lord if he could plant seeds. Allah said to him, "Do you not have whatever you wish?" That means: don't you already have everything you love? The man said, "Yes, I do, but I love to plant things."[^2]

So Allah let him. The man scattered his seeds, and the plants came up, and grew tall, and got ripe, and were harvested, all faster than the blink of an eye! And the crop piled up into great big heaps, like mountains.[^2][^3]

Then Allah said, "Take it, son of Adam," which means: here, it's yours, "for nothing ever fills you."[^2] People always want a little bit more.

The man from the desert said, "You'll only find a man like that among Quraysh or the Ansar, because they are farmers. We aren't farmers!" And the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, laughed.[^2]

Here in this world, we wait for days and days for a little plant to grow. In Jannah, the planting is done in a moment, with no hard work at all. And here we learn to be patient, and we thank Allah for what we have.

### Activity: Sow, Wait, and Share the Yield

<!-- activity:start audience="4-7" concept_id="lesson.057.activity.sow-wait-and-share-the-yield" -->

With your mom or dad: put some wet cotton in a cup, lay a few lentils or beans on top, and say, "Bismillah." Every morning, sprinkle a little water on it, and draw what you see in a small box on your paper. Count together how many sleeps you waited until the green leaf came out. When the sprouts are big, give a cup of them to your grandma or a neighbor, or, with your mom or dad, put out some seeds for the sparrows, and say, "This is charity, for Allah." Then, next to your boxes, draw a great big field in Jannah that grows in the blink of an eye.

### An Authentic Hadith: A Crop like Mountains in the Blink of an Eye

<!-- story:start audience="8-12" role="primary" type="hadith" source_id="bukhari-2348" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true report that al-Bukhari recorded in his Sahih. It is not a made-up story.**

<!-- retelling:start source_id="bukhari-2348" audience="8-12" -->

Abu Hurayrah, may Allah be pleased with him, tells us that one day the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, was talking with his Companions, and a Bedouin, one of the desert folk, was sitting with him.[^2]

The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said that a man among the people of Jannah asked his Lord's permission to farm. Imagine that: he is in Jannah, he has everything, and he still asks permission! Allah said to him, "Do you not have whatever you wish?" In other words: don't you already find everything you want? The man said, "Yes indeed, but I love to farm."[^2]

He didn't need the food. He just loved to scatter the seed with his own hand, as Ibn al-Qayyim explained.[^5] So Allah gave him permission, and he sowed. And here comes the surprise: the sprouting, the growing tall, the ripening, the harvesting and the gathering all happened faster than the blink of an eye! And the harvest piled up like mountains.[^2][^3]

Then Allah said, "Take it, son of Adam, for nothing ever fills you."[^2] The scholars said this shows that human beings were created loving to have lots, and that it points to how good it is to be content.[^3]

Then the Bedouin spoke up, honest and funny at the same time: "By Allah, you'll find he's none other than a man of Quraysh or of the Ansar, because they're farming people. As for us, we're not farming people!" And the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, laughed.[^2]

So what do we take from this hadith? In Jannah, the believer finds whatever he loves, even the fun of working with his own hands, with no tiredness and no waiting. And in this world, we plant patiently, we turn what we grow into charity, and we learn to say: "This is enough for me. Alhamdulillah."

## For Teens, Ages 13+

<!-- reader:start audience="13+" estimated_minutes="5.5" -->

<!-- unit:start id="13+.explanation" kind="explanation" -->

A newer phone. A sneaker drop that just landed. A follower count that never quite feels like enough. Every time you reach one thing, another one appears. You are not the only one who feels this. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, said in an authentic hadith that nothing ever fills the son of Adam, and Ibn Hajar commented: "It shows that souls are made to seek more and more of this world, and it points to the virtue of contentment."[^2][^3]

The striking thing is that this hadith describes a scene in Jannah itself, the place of which Allah said: {وَفِيهَا مَا تَشْتَهِيهِ الْأَنْفُسُ وَتَلَذُّ الْأَعْيُنُ}, "and therein is whatever souls desire and whatever delights the eyes."[^1] There, wanting more does no harm, because the giving never runs out. Here, wanting more with no limits just wears you out.

<!-- unit:end -->

<!-- terminology:start source_id="bukhari-2348" -->

- **`Asked his Lord's permission (ista'dhana rabbahu)`** — he asked Allah for leave: a courtesy that stays with the servant even in the Abode of Honor.[^2]
- **`Its heaping up (takwiruhu)`** — gathering it into heaps; the harvest was gathered up like mountains.[^3]
- **`The Bedouin (al-a'rabi)`** — someone who lives in the desert. In the hadith, the Bedouin said of his own people, "We are not farming people."[^2][^4]

### An Authentic Hadith: "But I Love to Farm"

<!-- story:start audience="13+" role="primary" type="hadith" source_id="bukhari-2348" authenticated="true" -->

**This is a true report that al-Bukhari recorded in his Sahih. It is not a made-up story.**

<!-- retelling:start source_id="bukhari-2348" audience="13+" -->

A gathering in Madinah. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, is speaking, and among those present is a Bedouin from the desert.[^2]

The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, tells of a man among the people of Jannah who asks his Lord's permission to farm. The question comes back to him: "Do you not have whatever you wish?" Isn't everything you wanted already yours? And he answers honestly: "Yes indeed, but I love to farm."[^2] He isn't complaining that something is missing. He simply loves to work with his hands. Ibn al-Qayyim said that perhaps he asked permission for a crop "that he would tend and sow with his own hand," though he had been spared its labor.[^5]

He sows, and the sprouting, the ripening, the readiness for harvest and the gathering all outrun the blink of an eye, and it stands like mountains.[^2] What takes the most skilled farmer on earth a whole season of uprooting, reaping, winnowing and gathering was finished there in "the space of a glance of the eye," as Ibn Hajar put it.[^3]

Then Allah says: "Take it, son of Adam, for nothing ever fills you."[^2] One sentence that lays human nature bare, just as it is, with no filter.

And the Bedouin speaks up, straight out: "By Allah, you'll find he's none other than a man of Quraysh or of the Ansar, for they are farming people; as for us, we are not farming people." And the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, laughs.[^2] The Bedouin wasn't putting on a show, and the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, didn't scold him. He laughed at his quick wit.[^4]

Now put yourself in front of this hadith. The craving for "more" isn't going to disappear. The real question is: where will you spend it? In Jannah, it is given without limit. Here, it has two guardrails: contentment that says, "This is enough for me," and planting for the Hereafter, which is never lost. The Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, told us that whatever a bird, a person or an animal eats from a Muslim's crop counts as charity for him.[^6]

### Discussion Questions

1. What is the difference between wanting more in Jannah and wanting more in this world?

2. Why does the craving for "more" wear a person out in this world? Give an example from your generation's life.

3. What does the man's answer, "Yes indeed, but I love to farm," reveal about the nature of the bliss of Jannah?

4. What do you learn from the Bedouin's frankness and from the Prophet's laughter, peace and blessings be upon him?

5. How can you turn an effort you are making right now into a "crop" that stays with you?

### Activity: Sow, Wait, and Share the Yield

<!-- activity:start audience="13+" concept_id="lesson.057.activity.sow-wait-and-share-the-yield" -->

Plant fast-growing seeds (arugula, mint or beans) and follow them for ten days. In a private note, record each day: the day number; the care you gave; one moment in your day when you felt the pull of "more" (buying something, upgrading something, comparing yourself to someone); and what you did with it. On day ten, share what you grew, or some grain for the birds, intending it as charity, without photographing it or posting it. Then write a paragraph: What did the waiting teach me? And what is the one thing I have decided to say "This is enough for me" about?

### Bedtime Du'a

<!-- bedtime-dua:start audience="13+" id="lesson.057.dua.guidance-piety-chastity-sufficiency" provenance="sunnah" source_id="muslim-2721a" attribution="prophetic" -->

A du'a the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, used to make, and a remedy for the soul that nothing ever fills:

> **اللَّهُمَّ إِنِّي أَسْأَلُكَ الْهُدَى وَالتُّقَى وَالْعَفَافَ وَالْغِنَى.**[^8]
>
> *"O Allah, I ask You for guidance, piety, chastity, and self-sufficiency."*
>
> What it means: O Allah, I ask You for guidance to the truth; for taqwa; for chastity (*al-'afaf*), which is keeping clear of what is not permitted and holding back from it; and for self-sufficiency (*al-ghina*), which here means richness of the soul and not needing people or what they own, as an-Nawawi explained.[^8][^9]

## Detailed Teaching Plans

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="adults" minutes="60" -->

### Adults — 60 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The learner narrates the hadith of the farmer (al-Bukhari 2348) in both its narrations and explains its key words (*at-tarf*, *al-istihsad*, *at-takwir*, *dunaka*); connects it to az-Zukhruf 43:71; presents what Ibn Hajar, Ibn al-Qayyim and al-Qari drew from it; explains the hadith of Anas (al-Bukhari 2320) and al-Qurtubi's reconciliation of it with the censure of amassing; and begins a real planting experiment with a fourteen-day log.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Copies of the lesson; a mushaf; a board with two columns headed "Farming in this world" and "Farming in Jannah"; fast-growing herb seeds, small pots and soil; the log table; an exit card.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher reads al-Bukhari 2348 and 7519 with *Fath al-Bari* in the Book of Sharecropping (*Kitab al-Muzara'ah*); the forty-sixth chapter of *Hadi al-Arwah*; al-Qari's commentary in *Mirqat al-Mafatih*; al-Bukhari 2320 with *Fath al-Bari*; and Ibn Kathir's tafsir of az-Zukhruf 43:71. The teacher keeps in mind that the saplings earned through dhikr have a lesson of their own and should not be expanded on here, and prepares cards for the stages of farming in this world (plowing, sowing, watering, sprouting, ripening, harvesting, winnowing, gathering).

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** Ask: "What is the longest you have ever waited for something to bear fruit in your life?" Then the learners put the farming-stage cards in order and estimate how long each stage takes.

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 20 minutes:** Read the ayah of az-Zukhruf with Ibn Kathir's tafsir (3 minutes); then the whole hadith in both narrations, pausing at the request for permission, the question "Do you not have whatever you wish?", the word *at-tarf*, Allah's words, and the Bedouin's remark (10 minutes). Then the statements of Ibn Hajar, Ibn al-Qayyim and al-Qari, showing that al-Qari's reading of rebuke and Ibn Hajar's reading of human nature meet in the virtue of contentment (4 minutes). Then the hadith of Anas with Ibn Hajar's comment (3 minutes). Fill in the two columns as you go.

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 10 minutes:** Discuss questions 3, 4 and 5. The teacher points out that eagerness for more is not blameworthy when it is directed at the Hereafter, and that contentment does not mean giving up work, since the farming itself is charity.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 15 minutes:** Each learner plants their seeds in a pot (7 minutes), sets up the log table (3 minutes), and decides who will receive the first yield and how, then pairs up with a partner to check in on each other (5 minutes).

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 10 minutes:** Exit card: "Name three lessons from the hadith of the farmer, and the one thing you will say 'This is enough for me' about this week." Then recite the du'a "O Allah, I ask You for guidance, piety, chastity, and self-sufficiency," with an-Nawawi's explanation of self-sufficiency, and agree to review the logs in two weeks.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For beginners, cover only the hadith, the ayah of az-Zukhruf and the hadith of Anas. Ask advanced learners to compare the wordings of 2348 and 7519, and to read what Ibn al-Munayyir said, as relayed by Ibn Hajar, about why the hadith belongs in the Book of Sharecropping.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Add nothing to the hadith in describing the farming of Jannah or how it happens; the most Ibn al-Qayyim said was: "I know of no mention of farming in Jannah except in this hadith." Do not rely on the report of 'Ikrimah about angels who bring the seed, since its chain is weak.[^10] Do not present al-Qari's reading of rebuke as the only meaning. Present the Prophet's laughter, peace and blessings be upon him, as evidence of his fine character, without acting out the scene.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="4-7" minutes="25" -->

### Children Ages 4-7 — 25 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The child retells that a man in Jannah loved to plant, and his crop came up and grew as big as mountains in the blink of an eye; says that the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, laughed at what the man from the desert said; plants lentils, draws them every morning, and gives some away as charity; and repeats the du'a for guidance, piety, chastity and self-sufficiency.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** A clear cup for each child; cotton; lentils or beans; a water spray bottle; a sheet with seven empty boxes; coloring pencils; a picture of a wheat field with no writing and no people; the du'a card.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher soaks a few seeds a day ahead to show the children, and prepares a cup in which lentils were sprouted a week earlier, for comparison. The teacher practices telling the report without adding anything to it.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 4 minutes:** Show a dry seed and the cup of sprouted lentils, and ask: "How many days do you think this one needed to look like that?"

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Reading the Evidence — 7 minutes:** Read the explanation and then the report. Ask the children to blink when you reach "faster than the blink of an eye," then show the picture of the field.

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 4 minutes:** Ask questions 1, 2 and 3, and teach the children the sentence: "Alhamdulillah, this is enough for me."

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 7 minutes:** Each child puts the cotton and seeds in their cup and says "Bismillah," draws the first box, and takes the cup and the sheet home to finish the drawings every morning with their family.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 3 minutes:** Ask: "Who will you give some of your sprouts to?" Then say the du'a together.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** For the youngest, three boxes and the sentence "In Jannah, crops grow in a moment" are enough. Ask the older ones to tell the report to a brother or sister in three sentences.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not draw the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, or the Companions, or any image of Allah, the Exalted, and do not act out the scene through role-play. Present the words "nothing ever fills you" as a description of human nature, not as a scolding of the child. Watch the children so they do not put seeds in their mouths or noses, and have them scatter seeds for the birds together with a parent.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="8-12" minutes="40" -->

### Children Ages 8-12 — 40 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The student narrates the hadith in order; puts in order the stages of the crop that took place in the blink of an eye; explains "outran the blink of an eye," "readiness for harvest" and "take it"; states that what a Muslim plants in this world is charity; and begins a seven-day log for an arugula plant, deciding what to share from it.

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Term cards; cups with soil or cotton, and arugula seeds; a seven-day table; farming-stage cards; a mushaf.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher writes az-Zukhruf 43:71 on the board and prepares cards for the five stages: sprouting, ripening, readiness for harvest, harvesting, gathering.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** Ask: "Have you ever waited a long time for something and wished you could speed it up?" Then the students put the stage cards in order and estimate how long each takes in this world.

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 12 minutes:** Read the explanation (3 minutes), then the story, pausing before the man's answer to ask, "What do you think he'll say?" (6 minutes), then the terms (3 minutes).

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 8 minutes:** Ask questions 1, 3 and 5. Explain that wanting more is part of human nature, that contentment keeps it in check, and that planting is charity.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 10 minutes:** Each student plants their seeds, fills in day one on the table, and decides who will get the arugula in a sandwich or where they will scatter seed for the birds.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 5 minutes:** Each student writes the sentence: "In this world, a crop needs ..., and in Jannah ..." Then recite the du'a.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** Give students who are struggling a table with ready-made drawings and blanks to fill in. Ask advanced students to write a paragraph on the difference between the wordings of the two narrations, or to memorize the hadith of Anas.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** Do not draw the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, or the Companions. Do not make fun of the Bedouin; explain that the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, laughed at his quick wit. Using scissors and making the sandwich happen under a parent's supervision, and the arugula is washed well before eating.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->

<!-- lesson-plan:start audience="13+" minutes="50" -->

### Teens, Ages 13+ — 50 Minutes

<!-- lesson-plan:outcomes -->
**Learning Outcomes:** The teen analyzes the words "nothing ever fills you" in light of Ibn Hajar's comments and distinguishes wanting more in Jannah from wanting more in this world; explains "asked his Lord's permission," "its heaping up" and "the Bedouin"; identifies an area of their life where they always ask for more; and begins a ten-day log that combines caring for a plant with tracking the pull of "more."

<!-- lesson-plan:materials -->
**Materials:** Copies of the lesson; a private sheet that is not collected; seeds and pots; three scenario cards.

<!-- lesson-plan:preparation -->
**Preparation:** The teacher prepares three scenarios: an ad for "the new model" of a phone; a friend comparing follower counts; and wanting to buy new clothes when you already have enough. The teacher also reads what Ibn Hajar and al-Qari said about the hadith.

<!-- lesson-plan:opening -->
**Opening — 5 minutes:** Ask: "Name something you thought would be enough once you got it. Was it?" Listen without commenting.

<!-- lesson-plan:evidence -->
**Studying the Evidence — 12 minutes:** Read the explanation and the story, pausing at three moments: the man's answer, Allah's words "Take it, son of Adam," and the Prophet's laughter, peace and blessings be upon him. Then read the terms.

<!-- lesson-plan:instruction -->
**Guided Instruction — 13 minutes:** Groups discuss the scenarios in light of questions 1 and 2, then answer question 5 in private writing. The teacher points out that contentment does not mean laziness, and that the praiseworthy kind of eagerness is eagerness for what lasts.

<!-- lesson-plan:activity -->
**Activity — 12 minutes:** Each student plants their seeds, designs their own log, and decides who they will share what they grow with.

<!-- lesson-plan:assessment -->
**Assessment and Closing — 8 minutes:** Each student writes a paragraph: "What will I say 'This is enough for me' about?" Then recite the du'a with an-Nawawi's explanation of self-sufficiency. After ten days, the teacher asks a general question about how the experiment went.

<!-- lesson-plan:differentiation -->
**Differentiation:** Students who find writing difficult may make a private audio recording instead. Ask advanced students for a short piece of research on al-Qurtubi's reconciliation of the hadith of Anas with the censure of amassing, as relayed by Ibn Hajar.

<!-- lesson-plan:safeguards -->
**Teaching Cautions:** The hadith must not be used to shame anyone for being poor or rich, or to judge anyone who owns new things. Discuss the pressures of shopping and comparison without naming names. The giving happens without photos or posting, and with the family's knowledge.

<!-- lesson-plan:end -->
